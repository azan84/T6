# Referee report: IEEE JBHI

**Manuscript:** "Topological Segmentation Error and Boundary-Condition Tuning in Computed Coronary FFR: A Controlled In Silico Study"
**Reviewer perspective:** medical image segmentation and imaging informatics (topology-aware learning, clDice, coronary centerline benchmarks), model credibility (ASME V&V40, FDA in silico evidence)
**Date:** 2026-10-09 (blind; manuscript, supplement and page images only)

## Summary

The authors insert synthetic cosine stenoses (40–80% DS) into 150 lesion instances (108 trees, 93 patients) from the ImageCAS-X test split. The selection is stratified across baseline-FFR bands of 0.65–0.95. They apply four segmentation errors one at a time: T1, deletion of the largest side branch distal to the lesion; T2, truncation of the host vessel 25 mm beyond the lesion; T3, lesion lengthened by 2.46 mm (inter-observer HD95); and T4, a uniform 7% radius reduction from the lesion onwards (derived from inter-observer DSC). Each corrupted tree is solved with a steady Poiseuille + Young–Tsai reduced-order model under four outlet protocols: (A) fixed clean conductances, (B) a re-derived Murray-type bed, (C) one global bed scaling tuned to clean territory flows, and (D) per-territory scaling. Two microvascular bed structures are used (discrete outlets, leaky distributed outflow). The outcomes are the decision flip at 0.80 and a "passes-and-wrong" rate: perfusion residual <10% while |ΔFFR| > 0.05. These are compared against repeat-FFR and simulated-physiology noise floors. One 3D OpenFOAM case checks direction. Under Protocol A, topological errors flip 32–33% of decisions against 6–10% for caliber errors. Re-derivation or tuning reduces this to 5–19%. After tuning, 19–20% (discrete) and 5–8% (leaky) of topological-error models, and 4–18% of caliber-error models (mostly taper), pass the perfusion check while being materially wrong. The central message is that a perfusion match after tuning does not certify the computed FFR.

The question matters to health informatics. The calibration-is-not-validation point is made cleanly and quantified, and the study is carefully executed and honestly reported. My main concerns are about representativeness. The error models do not yet show that they stand for real segmentation failures at realistic magnitudes. The topological and caliber errors are sized on asymmetric bases, so the headline "three to six times" ranking is partly built in. The informatics claims about overlap metrics and QC are asserted but not measured, even though the data needed to measure them is in hand.

## Strengths

1. **A clear, decision-level question.** The study judges segmentation error by the 0.80 treatment decision and benchmarks against the repeat-FFR noise floor ([30], [31]) rather than a continuous pressure error. This is the right currency for clinical relevance and is uncommon in the segmentation-uncertainty literature ([10]–[13]).
2. **The passes-and-wrong construct.** It operationalises a core V&V40 and in silico credibility principle: agreement with calibration data is not validation evidence. The concept is simple enough for non-CFD readers and is relevant to perfusion-calibrated digital twins ([8], [33]).
3. **A factorial design that separates confounders.** The same corrupted anatomy is solved under four BC protocols and two bed structures. Findings are claimed only where the direction agrees in both beds. Paired McNemar/Holm tests and a mixed-effects check are appropriate.
4. **Transparent reporting of denominators and exclusions.** Table S1 lists exclusions, convergence is reported for all 2,599 + 769 solves, and the 3D mesh checks report failed acceptance rules openly (Table S8). Fixed seeds and the selection algorithm are reported.
5. **Honest limitations.** The paper acknowledges that the cohort is stratified ("not clinical prevalences"), that RCA instances are largely missing from C/D, that the 3D baseline crosses 0.80 because of the radius definition, and that the targets are noise-free.
6. **The two prior "contradictory" findings ([20] vs [21]) are reconciled** as products of BC protocol. This is a useful synthesis.
7. **Demand replication with re-selected cohorts** (Table S5) answers the obvious low-flow objection rather than only scaling k on a fixed cohort.

## Weaknesses

### W1 (major): Topological and caliber errors are sized on asymmetric bases, so the headline ranking is partly built in by the design
- Anchor: II-C: "T1 and T2 have no measured magnitude and are design choices"; T1 "deletes the **largest** side branch that leaves the host vessel beyond the lesion"; T2 truncates 25 mm beyond the lesion. In contrast, "The caliber magnitudes are the inter-observer statistics … so each caliber error is as large as the disagreement between two annotators", and the limitations add "inter-observer statistics that likely overstate agreement".
- Problem: The topological errors are near worst-case. The largest distal side branch is typically a diagonal or obtuse marginal, and a break 25 mm past the lesion removes most of the run-off. The caliber errors are deliberately small, and the authors themselves say they are probably too small. The abstract and conclusion ("three to six times as often") present a ratio that depends on these two choices. A skilled reader will see this asymmetry immediately.
- Fix: (a) Add a magnitude sweep for T1 (deleted-branch rank or origin diameter, e.g. largest / median / smallest distal branch ≥1 mm) and T2 (break distance, e.g. 10/25/50 mm, or the fraction of distal bed lost). Report flip rate as a function of lost bed fraction, a single continuous "dose" that puts T1 and T2 on one axis. (b) Size at least one caliber error from a more realistic source than whole-tree inter-observer DSC, such as published CCTA-vs-QCA/IVUS stenosis-diameter discrepancy, or per-lesion inter-observer radius differences measured directly from the paired annotations. (c) Reword the abstract and conclusion so the ranking is explicitly conditional on these magnitudes, not only in the limitations.

### W2 (major): The error models are not anchored to real segmentation failures, although the dataset allows it
- Anchor: II-C, the derivation of T4 from DSC: "The DSC of 92.8% sets T4 as the radius ratio λ of two coaxial cylinders, 2λ²/(λ²+1)=0.928"; T3 from HD95.
- Problem: (i) Whole-tree DSC is volume-weighted, dominated by the proximal vessels, and mixes boundary misplacement with branch-presence disagreement. Inverting it to a uniform coaxial radius ratio is a strong and unvalidated assumption. (ii) HD95 over a coronary tree is driven mainly by distal branch-length and branch-presence disagreements, which are topological, not by lesion extent, so using it as a lesion-length error is a category mismatch. (iii) The caliber errors are one-sided (narrowing and lengthening only). CT segmentation errors at lesions are bidirectional, and calcium blooming typically *overestimates* stenosis while under-segmentation of the distal lumen narrows it. (iv) No throat-only (stenosis severity) caliber error is modelled, although throat radius enters the resistance to the fourth power and through the expansion-loss term, and is the caliber error most likely to move FFR across 0.80. T4 scales throat and distal lumen together, which is a different perturbation.
- Fix: The ImageCAS-X test split contains a second, independent annotation for every scan. Use annotator 2's lumen (or real outputs of one or two benchmark segmentation methods from [18]) as a *naturally occurring* error on the same instances. Report flip and passes-and-wrong rates for these real perturbations, and classify the observed differences as topological or caliber. This would also give empirical frequencies and sizes of missed branches and breaks to replace the design-choice magnitudes. If that is not feasible, at minimum add (a) a throat-only DS error of ±5–10 percentage points, (b) widening as well as narrowing for T3/T4, and (c) a justification of the DSC-to-radius and HD95-to-length mappings, or a replacement with per-lesion radius differences measured on the paired annotations.

### W3 (major): The informatics claim that overlap metrics do not detect the decisive errors is asserted, not measured
- Anchor: IV-B: "a missing side branch removes few voxels, so the error types that carried the decision risk here are the ones that the usual overlap metrics do not measure, although topology-aware measures such as clDice exist [32]"; IV-C: "check that the side branches beyond the lesion are present … because overlap scores do not reveal these errors". The conclusion repeats it: "a check of the branching near the lesion, which overlap scores miss".
- Problem: This is the main bridge to JBHI readers who build segmentation models, but the manuscript reports no DSC, HD95, clDice, Betti-number error or centerline overlap/overtraining metric (OV/OF/OT) for any corrupted mask. Deleting the *largest* distal side branch may reduce DSC by a non-trivial amount, so "few voxels" needs numbers. The masks and centerlines are already available, so this costs little.
- Fix: For every corrupted instance, compute DSC, HD95, clDice, the Betti-0/Betti-1 error (or a connected-component count) and centerline OV/OF against the clean mask. Present a small table or scatter of each metric against |ΔFFR| or flip, coloured by error type. Report the discrimination of each metric for flip (e.g. AUC). If DSC is insensitive while clDice/Betti detect T1/T2, the paper gains a quantitative, actionable QC result. If they do not, the claim should be withdrawn.

### W4 (major): Practical implications are qualitative; the paper stops short of a usable QC rule
- Anchor: IV-C: "the results suggest four considerations … These considerations follow from the results but are not validated decision rules"; "record the perfusion mismatch before tuning, which erases it: in the discrete bed only 22% of re-derived topological-error models passed".
- Problem: The data could support two concrete QC signals: (a) the pre-tuning perfusion residual under Protocol B as a detector of topological error, and (b) the fitted tuning factor itself (Protocol C scaling or Protocol D per-territory factors far from 1) as a red flag. Neither is evaluated, so a pipeline developer cannot act on "record the mismatch".
- Fix: Report sensitivity and specificity (or ROC/AUC) of the pre-tuning residual and of |log(tuning factor)| for detecting (i) the presence of a topological error and (ii) passes-and-wrong. Compare against the simulated noise floor, so the false-alarm rate on correct anatomy is known. State openly that the leaky bed makes (a) weak (89% of models pass).

### W5 (major for S3, wording): Abstract and Results lead with Protocol A, the least realistic pipeline
- Anchor: Abstract: "With fixed boundary conditions, branching (topological) errors changed the decision in 32–33% of models"; conclusion: "missed branches and vessel breaks changed the decision three to six times as often as caliber errors".
- Problem: Protocol A keeps the clean-anatomy conductances, a situation that an automated image-only pipeline cannot be in. Commercial and academic CT-FFR pipelines resemble Protocol B. Under B, topological flips are 5% in the leaky bed (Table S6), close to the 5.0–6.5% repeat-FFR floor, and the topological–caliber difference is not significant there (p = 0.18). The abstract mentions B only in pooled form, so a reader takes away the 32–33% figure as the risk.
- Fix: Give the Protocol B result equal prominence in the abstract and conclusion, e.g. "with boundary conditions re-derived from the segmented tree, as in automated pipelines, topological flips fell to 5–14%". Restrict "three to six times" to Protocol A explicitly. Also clarify the sentence "This represents the case in which the boundary conditions were obtained correctly despite the segmentation error": the bed of the deleted vessel is lost, so the BCs are not "correct". State when A arises in practice (e.g. outlet resistances from an independent atlas or prior study).

### W6 (minor–major): Perfusion "territory" definition is physiologically coarse, especially for the RCA
- Anchor: II-D: "A perfusion territory is the subtree below each child of the first bifurcation of the clean tree"; C/D undefined "for 36 of 96 and 49 of 149 vessel breaks, all but four in the RCA".
- Problem: For the left tree the first bifurcation approximates LAD vs LCx. For the RCA the first child is often the conus or an SA-nodal branch, so the "two territories" are a tiny branch against the rest. This explains why C/D are mostly undefined in the RCA, and makes the matched-flow check physiologically meaningless where it is defined. Clinical perfusion imaging reports AHA-segment or myocardial-territory flows, typically more than two regions per tree. The finding that per-territory tuning corrects the missed branch depends directly on territory granularity.
- Fix: State this mapping limitation in Methods. Ideally, add a sensitivity analysis with finer territories (e.g. subtrees at the second bifurcation level, or the vessel-to-segment mapping used in [8]) and report how passes-and-wrong under C/D changes. At minimum, report C/D results separately for LAD and LCx hosts.

### W7 (minor): 3D case is n = 1 and differs from the cohort model in radius definition
- Anchor: III-D: "The meshed lumen was wider than the radius used by the reduced-order model (median difference 0.14 mm …), and on the reduced-order radius the baseline was 0.761."
- Problem: This is acceptable as a direction check and is honestly reported. However, the abstract's "in one three-dimensional case it reduced a shift of 0.074 to −0.0007" reads as more than a single-case illustration. The 0.14 mm radius offset between distance-transform radius and marching-cubes surface is itself a segmentation-to-model pipeline error of the same order as T4 (7% of a ~2 mm radius). It also moved the baseline across 0.80 (0.761 vs 0.870), so it is a direct demonstration of the paper's thesis that is not exploited.
- Fix: Keep the 3D result out of the abstract or flag it as "a single 3D case". In the Discussion, briefly note that the radius-definition offset (distance map vs surface) is itself a pipeline-level caliber error large enough to flip this case. QC pipelines should fix one radius convention.

### W8 (minor): Figure clarity and accessibility
- Fig. 2: Each band point is based on very few models (bands are omitted only below three), yet lines join points that reach 100%. Add per-band n or Wilson intervals, or use markers without connecting lines. The legend omits Protocol D, which appears only in Table I, so add it or a note.
- Fig. 3: The mixed linear/log axis (symlog) is unusual. Mark the 0.01 break on the axis and make the 0.10 pass threshold a visible vertical line. Points overlap heavily, so consider transparency or a marginal histogram.
- Fig. 4: The rainbow (jet-like) colormap is not perceptually uniform or colour-blind safe, so use viridis/cividis. Panels (a)–(c) are small relative to (e)–(f).
- Fig. 1(d): The T3 and T4 curves overlap the "With lesion" curve almost completely at this scale. Add an inset or plot the difference from the lesioned radius.
- The term "taper" for T4 is misleading. It is a uniform radius scaling, not a change of taper slope, and the abstract's "narrowed taper" compounds this. Rename it ("distal under-segmentation" or "uniform narrowing").

### W9 (minor): Reproducibility below JBHI informatics norms
- Anchor: "The code is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request."
- Problem: The work is entirely computational on public data and the pipeline is fully scripted (S1). The instance list, corrupted trees and solver are the main reusable contribution, and the conclusion says "the same test can be adapted to other … pipelines". Code on request undercuts this.
- Fix: Deposit the code, the 150-instance list (scan ID, vessel, position, length, DS) and the per-instance results table in a public archive with a DOI. Report software versions for the reduced-order solver and statistics (the variational-inference package and settings).

### W10 (minor): Statistical and reporting details
- Table S2: "intervals are posterior mean ±1.96 posterior standard deviations from the variational approximation". Mean-field VI is known to underestimate posterior variance. Either refit with MCMC or note that the intervals are likely too narrow (the conclusions do not rest on them).
- The 13% and 16% sensitivity thresholds are not derived. State their origin (e.g. 1.0× and 1.3× the lower within-subject CV).
- IV-A: "After one global scaling, about half of the models that passed were materially wrong". The pass-rate denominator behind this is not given in any table, so add it to Table S3 or Table I.
- Table I: T2 flip denominators differ between A (60) and B (96) in the discrete bed, which makes A-vs-B rows hard to compare by eye. Add a paired-subset column or a footnote.
- The k × 0.7 / × 1.3 fixed-cohort sensitivity (Table S4) is never mentioned in the main text. Add one sentence in II-B or III-E.

**Overall: 6.4 / 10**

**Recommendation: Major revision.** The core finding (calibration to perfusion does not certify FFR, and BC protocol governs how segmentation errors propagate) is valuable and soundly computed. Acceptance requires the following. Anchor at least part of the error set to real segmentation discrepancies, using the second-annotator masks already in the dataset (W2). Add magnitude sweeps or a common lost-bed-fraction axis so the topological–caliber ranking is not built in (W1). Measure the overlap and topology metrics that the informatics conclusions rely on (W3). Rebalance the abstract toward the re-derived protocol that automated pipelines actually use (W5).
