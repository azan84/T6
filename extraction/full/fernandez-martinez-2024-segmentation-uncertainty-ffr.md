# fernandez-martinez-2024-segmentation-uncertainty-ffr

## Bibliographic

- Year: 2024 (received 6 Dec 2023; revised 20 Feb 2024; accepted 24 Mar 2024)
- Venue: International Journal for Numerical Methods in Biomedical Engineering, 40(6):e3822
- DOI: 10.1002/cnm.3822
- Code URL: NOT REPORTED

## Problem & claim
- Imaging modality and domain: Coronary CT angiography (CCTA) of coronary arteries (RCA, LAD, LCX), cross-referenced against invasive FFR from coronary catheterization; patient-specific coronary hemodynamics (FFRCT).
- Task: Other — uncertainty quantification of FFRCT with respect to the CCTA lumen-segmentation threshold, via 3D–0D coupled CFD simulation, not a segmentation or classification model itself.
- Stated gap the paper attacks (Introduction): "it remains unclear under which conditions this uncertainty becomes more apparent and how it evolves along the coronary tree. Therefore, we see the need to systematically analyze this geometry-related uncertainty due to operator dependency during the segmentation process." The paper explicitly positions itself against two prior works — Venugopal et al. (three-operator manual segmentation, steady-state, local pressure drop only) and Uus et al. (single patient, distal lesion, threshold variations of 22.8%–40%, "doubles the inter-operator variability (11%) reported in the literature ... so the question remains open for minor, and more clinically relevant, degrees of uncertainty").
- Central claim in one sentence: Minimal, clinically realistic intra-operator (6%) and inter-operator (15%) global segmentation-threshold variations produce FFRCT errors that grow with distal location and stenosis severity — large enough near the 0.80 cutoff to plausibly flip a revascularization decision in a distal, severe-lesion patient (P9), while proximal/mild-to-moderate mid-segment lesions remain within a "safe window."

## Method
- Architecture / algorithm, in enough detail to reimplement at a block level:
  1. **Segmentation (Sec. 2.2):** 3D Slicer, hybrid thresholding + seeded region-growing on the full aortocoronary domain. "In this work, we determined the optimum threshold for each patient based on the criteria of two medical experts, an interventionist cardiologist and a radiologist, who validated the limits of the coronary lumens. All threshold values ranged between 180 and 250 HU over the 14 CT scans." A single consensus "optimal" threshold per patient is the baseline; no independent from-scratch segmentations are produced.
  2. **Uncertainty injection (Sec. 2.2):** "Once the optimal threshold was determined, intra and inter-operator threshold variations were performed." Each patient's optimal HU threshold is shifted by fixed relative percentages (±6% for intra-operator, ±15% for inter-operator), producing 4 additional geometries per patient (intra+, intra−, inter+, inter−) alongside the optimal one, all from the *same* base CT image and *same* thresholding/region-growing pipeline — only the scalar HU cutoff changes.
  3. **CFD (Sec. 2.3):** Finite volume, ANSYS Fluent, incompressible unsteady Navier–Stokes, laminar, Newtonian, rigid wall. Domain truncated to the relevant left- or right-coronary sub-tree (≥1 mm diameter vessels incl. first-generation side branches) to reduce cost.
  4. **Inlet BC:** Patient-specific 8-term Fourier fit to catheterization-measured proximal pressure; where unavailable (50% of patients) a scaled standard aortic waveform tuned to the patient's systolic/diastolic pressures, "later validated through the corresponding measured FFR value."
  5. **Outlet BC:** 5-element RCRCR lumped-parameter model per outlet (Kim et al. 2010), implicitly coupled via UDFs; resistances/capacitances derived from myocardial-volume-based allometric scaling of total flow (assumed 4.5% of cardiac output), a fixed 70%/30% left/right split, and Murray's-law-based distribution of resistance among distal outlets (Ri ∝ di^−2.6); capacitances proportional to outlet area; hyperemic rescaling of resistance (×0.24 total) and resistor sub-ratios per prior work.
  6. **Mesh:** Hybrid conformal, triangular surface + 5 inflation layers + tetrahedral core, 850k–1.3M cells; mesh-independence check (doubling cells) changed stenosis pressure drop by <2%; quasi-periodic convergence over 6 cardiac cycles (3–5 h on a 56-core AMD-7453 cluster, 512 GB RAM).
- Key technical novelty vs. closest prior work: broadens Venugopal et al. (3D–0D transient vs. their steady-state, whole-tree pressure vs. their local pressure drop) and Uus et al. (14 patients spanning proximal/mid/distal and moderate/severe stenoses, at literature-realistic 6%/15% magnitudes, vs. their single patient at 22.8%/40%).
- Loss functions, training regime, augmentation, pre-training: NOT REPORTED / not applicable — no learned model; segmentation is a manual thresholding + region-growing pipeline, and hemodynamics is a first-principles CFD/0D solve.
- Inputs/outputs, dimensionality, resolution: Input — CCTA from a 64-detector GE LightSpeed VCT (SnapShot Segment Mode), slice thickness 0.625 mm, rotation time 0.4 s, 120 kV, collimation 0.625 mm, pitch 0.18–0.26. Output — per-case FFRCT (continuous, 0–1) at the same distal measurement point used clinically, for 14 patient-specific models × 5 threshold conditions (optimal, ±6%, ±15%), plus 6 synthetic "dummy" location×severity combinations built on Patient 10's anatomy.

## Data
- Datasets used, with size, source, licence, public/private: Single-center study, 15 patients enrolled with isolated coronary artery disease, Cardiology Service, University Hospital of Badajoz, Spain (ethics-approved, Declaration of Helsinki, informed consent). One patient excluded for motion artifact, leaving **14 patient-specific coronary models** (RCA, LAD, LCX lesions; Table 1 lists age, sex, stenosis location, % diameter reduction, invasive FFR, FFRCT per case). Private clinical cohort; not publicly released; no licence stated.
- Annotation protocol, number of annotators, inter-rater agreement: Two clinical experts (an interventionist cardiologist and a radiologist) jointly set the single "optimal" threshold per patient by consensus — this is a threshold-selection consensus, not independent parallel segmentations. **No independent multi-operator segmentation was performed within this study, and no segmentation-level agreement metric (Dice, Hausdorff distance, diameter delta) is reported anywhere in the text.** The 6% and 15% uncertainty magnitudes are not measured from these two experts; see T6 Q2 below — they are taken from the external literature (refs 30, 39).
- Train/val/test split strategy; external validation present?: Not an ML train/test study. No held-out or external-center validation cohort — the paper's own Limitations state the entire cohort is single-center (see below).

## Evaluation
- Metrics used, with exact reported values (see T6 Q6 for the full quantitative catalogue). Headline: dummy-model maxima at distal+severe location — intra-operator ΔFFRCT = −0.032 (dummy) / −0.029 (population, Patient 9), inter-operator ΔFFRCT = −0.101 (dummy) / −0.079 (population, Patient 9); benchmark for "relevant" error is the invasive-FFR repeatability figure of 0.03 (cited from Sankaran et al. 2016, ref 22, in Sec. 3): "the error in the repeatability of the invasive FFR (0.03) is taken as the benchmark."
- Baselines compared against: The paper's own "optimal segmentation" FFRCT is the reference; invasive FFR is the outer clinical reference (Table 1); prior published threshold-uncertainty studies (Sankaran et al., Uus et al., Venugopal et al., Agujetas et al.) are compared narratively, not re-run.
- Statistical testing / confidence intervals / ablations: None reported — no formal statistical test (no p-values, no CI) is applied to the FFRCT differences; results are reported as point deltas per case/threshold and interpreted graphically (Figures 7, 8, 13, 14). A mesh-independence "ablation" (cell-count doubling) is reported (<2% pressure-drop change).
- Compute reported: No GPU; CPU cluster — "56-core AMD-7453 cluster provided with 512 GB RAM," 3–5 h per case to quasi-periodic convergence over 6 cardiac cycles. Mesh size 850k–1.3M cells. No parameter counts (not a learned model).

## Reproducibility
- Code released? Weights released? Data released? Licence: No code repository given (commercial Fluent + 3D Slicer + in-house UDFs). No data release (private, available "on request from the corresponding author," restricted by privacy/ethics). No licence stated for any artifact.
- Anything that would block reimplementation: Proprietary commercial CFD solver (ANSYS Fluent) with custom UDFs for the 0D RCRCR coupling (equations 2–9 given in the paper, so the 0D model itself is reimplementable); patient-specific pressure waveforms and CT images are private clinical data not released; exact per-patient threshold values, mesh files, and UDF code are not supplied as supplementary material in the extracted text.

## Limitations
- Limitations the authors admit (Sec. 3.3, "Limitations," quoted in full): "Although this is a pilot study with a small number of cases, we think its number is reasonable for the results provided in the present work. Nevertheless, the generalizability of the study is limited as the geometries presented in this work belong to the same center. A multicenter collaboration would have allowed us to enrich this study by assessing the influence of the segmentation process in different scanners with dissimilar accuracy and broadening geometrical variabilities like shape-changing lumens or different branching patterns."
- Additional caveats admitted elsewhere in the text (not under the "Limitations" heading): rigid-wall assumption because "in FDA-approved solutions the fluid–solid interaction still needs to be addressed" (Sec. 2.3.1); fixed 70%/30% left/right coronary flow split validated only because "all patients in this study have right dominance" — a dynamic, dominance-dependent split "has been proposed to improve the simulation accuracy" but was not used (Sec. 2.3.3); "In light of higher variances in the segmentation threshold, greater variations in the FFRCT should be expected" (Sec. 3.2) — i.e., their 6%/15% figures are explicitly a lower bound, not a worst case; the discussion notes reference-pressure measurement location matters: "reference distal pressure measurements taken at the terminal ends of the fluid domain should be avoided" and flags an unresolved domain-truncation trade-off (Sec. 3.2).
- Limitations I can see that they do NOT admit: (1) The 6%/15% uncertainty magnitudes are imported from two unrelated external studies (refs 30, 39) on plaque/lumen quantification, not measured on this paper's own 14 patients or by its own two experts — yet the paper's own conclusions ("minimum uncertainty that trained professionals can achieve") are stated as if empirically grounded in this cohort. (2) The "intra-" and "inter-operator" labels are applied to what is structurally a single manipulation (a global HU-threshold shift of differing magnitude) — no actual second operator, no actual repeat segmentation by the same operator, and no segmentation-level agreement metric were ever produced or reported, so the paper cannot rule out that real independent operators produce spatially non-uniform (not globally rescaled) lumen differences. (3) No formal reclassification/flip-rate analysis across the 14 cases relative to the 0.80 cutoff is performed — the closest thing is a narrative flag on a single patient (P9). (4) Boundary-condition retuning via Murray's law is applied identically and automatically to every threshold variant with no untuned control condition, so the paper cannot separate how much of the reported (small) FFRCT sensitivity is because the outlet resistances were re-balanced to the new geometry versus what would happen if BCs were left fixed after a geometry perturbation. (5) No statistical inference (no p-values/CI) is applied to any of the reported deltas.

## Openings
- Explicit "future work" / forward-looking statements, quoted: "A multicenter collaboration would have allowed us to enrich this study by assessing the influence of the segmentation process in different scanners with dissimilar accuracy and broadening geometrical variabilities like shape-changing lumens or different branching patterns" (Sec. 3.3). "Refining automatic segmentation methods and integrating numerical and data-driven approaches can mitigate input uncertainties" (Sec. 3.2, citing ref 58). "Those three simplifications [geometry, wall properties, lumped-parameter model] can fully or partially impact the elasticity effect, so those conclusions need further validation in future studies" (Sec. 2.3.1, re: wall elasticity).
- 3–6 unresolved questions this paper leaves open, each one sentence:
  1. Whether real independent-operator segmentations (rather than a single global HU-threshold rescale) produce spatially structured lumen errors (e.g., localized at the stenosis, at a side branch, or along stenosis length) whose FFRCT impact differs qualitatively from a uniform threshold shift.
  2. Whether the 6%/15% magnitudes, imported from other plaque-quantification studies, actually match the disagreement this paper's own two experts (or any other pair of operators) would show if they independently re-segmented the same 14 patients.
  3. Whether fixing (not re-tuning) the outlet 0D boundary conditions after a segmentation-induced geometry change would reveal a larger, currently masked, FFRCT sensitivity — the paper always re-tunes via Murray's law and never reports an untuned comparison.
  4. Whether a formal reclassification/flip-rate metric across all 14 (or more) cases, rather than a single narratively flagged patient (P9), would show a materially different picture of clinical risk near the 0.80 cutoff.
  5. Whether distinct, non-global error types (eccentric/off-axis lumen reconstruction, missed side branches, taper/undersizing along a stenosis) would rank differently from one another in their effect on FFRCT, a comparison this paper never attempts since it only varies one error type (global threshold) in magnitude.
  6. Whether multicenter/multi-scanner variability (explicitly named as future work) would push the "minimal" uncertainty envelope well beyond the 6%/15% figures used here.

## T6 decisive questions

**1. Exactly how was segmentation uncertainty generated? Single global HU-threshold shift, or distinct spatially-structured error types?**
It is a **single global HU-threshold shift applied to the whole lumen**, not distinct spatially-structured error types. Section 2.2 (CCTA image segmentation): "In this work, we determined the optimum threshold for each patient based on the criteria of two medical experts... All threshold values ranged between 180 and 250 HU over the 14 CT scans. Once the optimal threshold was determined, intra and inter-operator threshold variations were performed." The mechanism is one scalar HU cutoff, uniformly perturbed by a fixed relative percentage (±6%, ±15%) and re-run through the identical thresholding + region-growing pipeline: "Figure 3 shows the geometries of a patient's epicardial vessel generated with the optimal threshold (200 HU) and its intra (12 HU) and inter (30 HU) operator variations. As can be observed, the geometric variations are hardly distinguishable." The Results section explicitly calls this "the global threshold segmentation" (Sec. 3.2: "...the influence of the evolution of uncertainty on the FFRCT due to the global threshold segmentation based on the study population..."). No stenosis-length-specific, eccentric/off-axis, missed-side-branch/topological, or taper/undersizing error type is modeled — every case is a globally rescaled (grown or shrunk) version of the same lumen surface.

**2. Where do the 6% intra-operator and 15% inter-operator figures come from — real operators measured, or assumed/literature values?**
They are **literature values, not measured on this study's own patients or operators**. Section 2.2, verbatim: "An experienced radiologist's variation in terms of the segmentation threshold when segmenting the same images 2 months apart is around 6%.30 This intra-operator variability can be regarded as the lower bound of the threshold uncertainty. Regarding inter-operator variations, previous studies have shown a larger difference between the thresholds used by two radiologists when they extract lumens and plaques of the same arteries. This difference ranges from 11%30 to 22%.39 Here, we opt for a variation of 15% (an intermediate value between those references)." Reference 30 is Schepis et al. 2010 (Heart) and reference 39 is Leber et al. 2006 (JACC) — both external plaque/lumen-quantification studies, unrelated to the 14 patients in this paper. This is **decisive against T6's "real disagreement" claim**: Fernández-Martínez et al. did not measure any inter-segmenter disagreement themselves; they imported a percentage range from other authors' other cohorts and picked "an intermediate value" (15%) by choice, not by measurement.

**3. How many operators, how many segmentations each, is any inter-operator agreement metric reported?**
Two clinical experts are named — "two medical experts, an interventionist cardiologist and a radiologist" — but they performed **one joint/consensus determination of the optimal threshold per patient**, not independent parallel segmentations. No second, independently-produced segmentation by either expert (or a third party) exists anywhere in the study. **No inter-operator agreement metric (Dice, Hausdorff distance, diameter delta) is reported anywhere in the text** — the ±6%/±15% "operator" geometries are synthetic HU perturbations of the single consensus segmentation, not outputs of a second segmenter.

**4. Boundary-condition tuning passage (Murray's law resistance update per threshold) — quoted in full. Patient-specific measured target, or allometric rule with no measured target?**
Quoted in full (Sec. 2.3.2): "Specific resistances, Ri, and capacitances, Ci, must be carefully assigned in this model to perform a patient-specific simulation. Ri are obtained as follows: (i) Total myocardial volume is calculated by CCTA images, (ii) total mean resting coronary flow is estimated from that volume following an allometric scaling law, assuming that it approximately represents the 4.5% of the cardiac output, (iii) 70% (30%) of this flow rate is assigned to the left (right) coronary artery, (iv) total left (right) coronary resistance is calculated as the ratio between the mean pressure at the coronary inlets and the left (right) estimated flow rate, (v) the resulting resistances are further distributed among the coronary outlets based on the generalization of Murray's law, that is, by computing the resistance Ri of each distal branch following a power-law relationship between flow and vessels diameters: Qi ∝ di^2.6. Since the flow rate is inversely proportional to the resistance, Ri can be estimated as Ri ∝ di^−2.6. Ci, on the other hand, are selected to be proportional to the outlet areas... This tuning process was performed automatically for each patient segmentation threshold because branch diameters differ depending on the threshold considered. According to Murray's law, the vessel-specific resistances change with their diameter, and an update is required."
This is a **hybrid, and the retuning step itself is purely allometric/diameter-based with no re-measured patient-specific target**. The *total* coronary resistance uses a patient-specific measured mean inlet pressure (from catheterization-derived Fourier fit, or a scaled generic waveform for 50% of patients) divided by an *allometrically estimated* (not measured) total flow. But the per-threshold **update triggered by a geometry change** — the part relevant to T6 — is exclusively the Murray's-law diameter reallocation ("the vessel-specific resistances change with their diameter, and an update is required"): it redistributes a fixed total resistance/flow budget among outlets based on their new diameters, with **no re-measured pressure or flow target used to validate or correct the retuned model against the corrupted geometry**. There is no patient-specific measured target (e.g., a re-checked invasive pressure) used per threshold variant — the same original invasive/scaled waveform and total-flow estimate are kept fixed across all 5 threshold conditions; only the *distribution* of resistance across outlets changes via Murray's law.

**5. Do the authors test whether BC re-tuning MASKS the geometric error — tuned vs untuned comparison?**
**No. This is not done anywhere in the paper.** The Murray's-law retuning is applied automatically and identically to every one of the 5 threshold conditions for every patient — there is no reported "untuned" control (i.e., no simulation in which the outlet resistances from the optimal-threshold geometry are kept fixed while the corrupted/shifted geometry is run). The only sentence addressing the retuning's necessity is procedural, not comparative: "This tuning process was performed automatically for each patient segmentation threshold because branch diameters differ depending on the threshold considered... an update is required." No masking/absorption analysis, ablation, or tuned-vs-untuned outcome comparison is quoted or implied anywhere in Results, Discussion, or Limitations.

**6. What outcomes are reported — continuous deltas only, or per-case reclassification? All numeric FFR deltas and flip counts. How many of the 14 models sat near the threshold?**
**Continuous FFRCT deltas only; no formal per-case reclassification/flip-count table is reported.** All reported numeric deltas (ΔFFRCT relative to the optimal-threshold case), from the text:
- Dummy model (Patient 10 anatomy), by location × severity (Sec. 3.1): Proximal-moderate: optimal 0.924, "maximum difference of 0.005 under the inter-operator variations." Proximal-severe: optimal 0.879, inter-operator "reaching 0.019." Mid-moderate (= actual Patient 10): optimal 0.832 ("smaller variations," no single number given). Mid-severe: optimal 0.782, "+0.021 in the case of −15% HU and −0.023 in the case of +15% HU" [as printed; location of ± sign vs. threshold direction is ambiguous/possibly transposed in the extracted text but quoted verbatim]. Distal-moderate: "+0.035 at −15% and up to −0.057 at +15%." Distal-severe: "practically doubles, reaching −0.101 in the inter-operator case +15%," with intra-operator "equaling the benchmark of 0.03 for the case of +6%."
- Population extension (Sec. 3.2): "the maximum error was found in a distal, severe lesion, reaching a 0.029 and 0.079 mismatch for intra and inter-operator variations, respectively" (Patient 9). Dummy-model maxima given as upper bound: "0.032 and 0.101, respectively." Patient 12 (severe, mid-segment) is the first (only) case in the population where the "0.03 benchmark is exceeded" on the full ±15% inter-operator variation; Patient 9's intra-operator variation "is almost reached" (0.029 vs 0.03 benchmark).
- Reclassification/flip framing is narrative and limited to **one** patient: "Patient 9 represents the most critical scenario in the patient population. The patient has a severe distal stenosis localized in the LAD, and the invasive FFR value is 0.8, just at the cutoff to decide an intervention. Figure 12 shows that a minimal threshold-based variation in the geometry could lead to a wrong clinical decision regarding revascularization." Table 1 shows only Patient 9 has an invasive FFR essentially at the 0.80 cutoff (FFR=0.8, FFRCT=0.803); no other patient's FFR/FFRCT (range 0.73–0.96) is described as being near 0.80. **NOT REPORTED**: an explicit count of "how many of the 14 models sat near the threshold" beyond this single qualitative flag on Patient 9, and NOT REPORTED: any tabulated flip count (e.g., "N of 14 cases crossed 0.80 under ±6%/±15%").

**7. Are different error TYPES compared or ranked against each other?**
**No, they are not.** The paper varies only one error type — a global HU-threshold shift — at two magnitudes (6%, 15%) and in two directions (±), and studies how its effect changes with **stenosis location and severity** (a confound/moderator, not a different error type): "Both factors [location and degree of severity] must be considered when interpreting FFRCT computations." There is no comparison to, e.g., an eccentricity error, a topological/missed-branch error, a stenosis-length error, or a taper/undersizing error — those are outside this paper's scope entirely; only magnitude and direction of the same threshold-based error are varied.

**8. Own stated limitations and future work, verbatim.**
Section 3.3, "Limitations," verbatim, in full: "Although this is a pilot study with a small number of cases, we think its number is reasonable for the results provided in the present work. Nevertheless, the generalizability of the study is limited as the geometries presented in this work belong to the same center. A multicenter collaboration would have allowed us to enrich this study by assessing the influence of the segmentation process in different scanners with dissimilar accuracy and broadening geometrical variabilities like shape-changing lumens or different branching patterns." Forward-looking statements elsewhere: "Refining automatic segmentation methods and integrating numerical and data-driven approaches can mitigate input uncertainties" (Sec. 3.2); "Those three simplifications can fully or partially impact the elasticity effect, so those conclusions need further validation in future studies" (Sec. 2.3.1, re: rigid-wall assumption).

**9. DECISIVE JUDGEMENT — which of T6's (a)-(d) are already covered, partially covered, or not covered.**

- **(a) Draw segmentation error magnitudes from REAL inter-segmenter disagreement at matched segmentation quality — PARTIALLY COVERED, and weakly so.** The 6%/15% magnitudes are grounded in real, literature-reported operator threshold variability, but that variability was measured in *other* studies on *other* cohorts (Schepis 2010, ref 30; Leber 2006, ref 39), not on this paper's own 14 patients, and not by actual repeated/independent segmentation within this study. No segmentation-quality-matching procedure, and no agreement metric (Dice/Hausdorff/diameter) exists here at all. T6's ambition to draw error magnitudes from *its own* measured real inter-segmenter disagreement, at matched quality, with a reported agreement metric, goes beyond what this paper does — this paper borrows a number, it does not measure disagreement.

- **(b) Inject DISTINCT error types (stenosis-length, eccentric/off-axis, missed side-branch/topological, taper/undersizing) rather than a single global parameter — NOT COVERED.** This paper uses exactly the single global parameter T6 explicitly wants to move beyond: a uniform HU-threshold rescaling of the whole lumen. No spatially-structured or topologically distinct error type is modeled anywhere.

- **(c) RANK error types by effect on the binary FFR ≤0.80 decision-flip rate — NOT COVERED.** There is only one error type here (varied in magnitude/direction), so no ranking across types is possible or attempted. Outcome reporting is continuous-delta with a single narrative near-threshold flag (Patient 9); no formal flip-rate metric across the 14 cases is computed for even this one error type, let alone compared across types.

- **(d) Test whether re-tuning outlet BCs after a geometry change ABSORBS/MASKS the error — NOT COVERED.** Murray's-law-based resistance retuning is performed automatically and identically for every threshold variant as a built-in step of the pipeline, with no reported untuned control condition and no comparison of tuned-vs-untuned outcomes for the same corrupted geometry. The paper cannot speak to, and does not raise, the masking/absorption question at all.

**Net conclusion:** This paper is the closest prior work on FFRCT sensitivity to segmentation/threshold uncertainty, but it only weakly overlaps with T6's contribution (a) (via a borrowed literature magnitude, not a measured one) and does not touch (b), (c), or (d) at all. All four of T6's planned contributions remain substantively novel relative to this paper; (a) is the only one requiring some acknowledgment/positioning, since it must be argued that "measuring real inter-segmenter disagreement directly" (T6) is a materially different and stronger evidentiary basis than "importing a plausible percentage range from unrelated cohorts" (this paper).
