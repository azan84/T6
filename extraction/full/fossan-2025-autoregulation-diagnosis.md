## Problem & claim
- Imaging modality and domain: Coronary CT angiography (CCTA)-derived epicardial anatomy combined with invasive intracoronary pressure measurements (rest Pd/Pa and hyperemic FFR); coronary artery disease / CT-FFR diagnostic modeling.
- Task (segmentation / registration / detection / classification / generation / other): Other — development and validation of a lumped-parameter outlet boundary-condition (microvascular resistance) model for a reduced-order coronary blood-flow solver used to predict CT-FFR, contrasted against invasive FFR as reference.
- Stated gap the paper attacks: "Most current CT-FFR models assume a fixed microvascular response to adenosine, treating vasodilation capacity as constant and optimal for all patients and lesions." "current methods to model hyperemia in CT-FFR prediction assume that the microcirculation reacts predictably to adenosine, where the hyperemic microvascular resistance can be estimated directly from basal levels by assuming a fourfold reduction in resistance (10, 15–18). However, the assumption of the predictability of the microvascular response is largely based on a study on healthy individuals (19), and hence has limited justification based on knowledge of coronary pathophysiology and regulation." "The presence of relevant autoregulation mechanisms occurring in stenosed coronary arteries has been documented in vivo (3–5), but the relevance of autoregulation in CT-FFR prediction has not been studied."
- Central claim in one sentence: "We hypothesized that a CT-FFR model that incorporates patient-specific microvascular autoregulation better reflects the true hemodynamic impact of a stenosis than models using fixed resistance assumptions," and the resulting CT-FFRautoregulation model reduced prediction variability (SD 0.1006 vs. 0.1167, P = 0.013) and improved R² (0.398 vs. 0.179) relative to the fixed-response model against invasive FFR, "However, the area under the curve (AUC) remained unchanged."

## Method
- Architecture / algorithm, in enough detail to reimplement at a block level:
  - Base flow solver: "The pressure and flow distributions in the coronary networks were solved by a previously validated machine learning-augmented reduced-order model (23). The coronary network was divided into healthy segments, stenoses, and junctions. Pressure drops across each segment were first estimated using a simplified [one-dimensional(1-D)/zero-dimensional (0-D)] physics-based model. These estimates were then refined using a neural network trained to capture complex three-dimensional (3-D) fluid dynamics." (Full model in ref. 23: Fossan et al. 2021, CMAME.)
  - CT-FFR prediction pipeline (4 steps, "CT-FFR prediction" section, adopting the strategy of ref. 25):
    1. Estimate resting coronary flow and distribute it among the Nout outlets: Qout,l, l = 1,...,Nout.
    2. Perform a resting-state simulation with prescribed inlet pressure Pinlet and the prescribed outlet flows from step 1, to find Prest_out,l for each outlet.
    3. Compute outlet resistances: "Rrest_out,l = (Prest_out,l − Pv)/Qrest_out,l, with l = 1,...,Nout" (Eq. 2), where Pv = 5 mmHg (fixed reference venous pressure) throughout.
    4. Perform a hyperemic-state simulation with prescribed inlet pressure and prescribed outlet resistances computed as "Rhyp_out,l = Rrest_out,l / k, with l = 1,...,Nout" (Eq. 3), where k is the factor by which peripheral resistance drops from rest to hyperemia.
  - Resting flow allocation (geometry/mass-based, not hemodynamically solved): "Qcor = f · TMM," f = 0.8 mL/min/g (normal myocardial perfusion factor, ref. 26); "TMM = 2.4 · LVM" (total myocardial mass from segmented left-ventricle mass, ref. 25); "Qout,l = ml · Qcor," where ml is the fraction of myocardial mass supplied by outlet l, "estimated by the Voronoi algorithm (27, 28)." Inlet pressure from brachial cuff: "Pinlet = (1/3)SBP + (2/3)DBP" (Eq. 4).
  - Two competing models for k (the rest→hyperemia resistance-reduction factor), "Incorporation of autoregulation mechanisms in CT-FFR prediction":
    - Fixed-response model: "kfixed = c" (Eq. 6) — a single constant for all vessels/patients, optimized once by 10-fold cross-validation to maximize R² against invasive FFR.
    - Autoregulation model: "kautoregulation = 1 + (khealthy − 1) · e^{bx}, x = 1 − Pd/Pa(rest,out,l)" (Eq. 7) [sign of the exponent is garbled by PDF extraction; the text explicitly states the intent is decay: "the reduction of the resistance from rest to hyperemia was designed to exponentially decay for increasing pressure loss in the resting condition" — i.e., the functional form decays toward k=1 (no vasodilatory response) as x increases (larger resting pressure drop / more severe stenosis)]. khealthy = the microvascular response "before any exhaustion due to autoregulation (i.e., for vessels without any pressure loss Pd/Pa = 1 and x = 0 and, such that kautoregulation = khealthy)." b is "a decay factor that controls how the microvascular response drops for increasing severity of the disease" and was fixed to b = 4 during optimization ("Fixation of b resulted in more reliable predictions, and the value (b = 4) was specified through an initial optimization procedure"); only khealthy (analogous to c) was optimized per fold.
  - Independent empirical derivation of the microvascular response from invasive data (used to motivate/fit the model above), "Quantification of the microvascular response to adenosine": the flow through outlets affected by the stenosis was adjusted in both rest and hyperemic states to match invasive Pd/Pa (rest) and FFR (hyperemia); then "kinvasive = Rstenosis_microcirculation|rest,matched / Rstenosis_microcirculation|hyp,matched" (Eq. 1). "A kinvasive value of 4 reflects a textbook value for a normal reduction of the microvascular resistance from rest to hyperemia (i.e., healthy), whereas a value of 1 reflects no change in resistance (i.e., no microvascular response/vasodilation)."
- Key technical novelty vs. closest prior work: replacing a population-constant hyperemic-resistance-reduction factor (the prevailing fourfold-reduction assumption, refs. 10, 15–18) with a lesion-specific factor that is an explicit function of the resting pressure drop across the stenosis (autoregulation-aware), derived from and validated against invasive pressure data in diseased (not only healthy) vessels.
- Loss functions, training regime, augmentation, pre-training: Not a trained neural model in this paper (the neural-network refinement is inherited unchanged from ref. 23). Model calibration here is parameter optimization (c, khealthy) via "10-fold randomized cross-validation ... by maximizing the coefficient of determination (R²score) between the predicted and invasive FFR."
- Inputs/outputs, dimensionality, resolution: Inputs — CCTA-segmented coronary lumen geometry and left-ventricle mass, brachial cuff SBP/DBP, invasive Pd/Pa (rest, subset) and FFR (hyperemia, all). Outputs — per-lesion CT-FFR (continuous, cutoff ≤0.80 = hemodynamically significant), for 280 lesions in 156 patients (171 of these lesions also have resting Pd/Pa for the kinvasive derivation).

## Limitations
- Limitations the authors admit (verbatim, "Limitations" section):
  - "The number of patients and lesions included in this study was limited, preventing a proper multivariate analysis, which could potentially have revealed other relationships between patient characteristics and the microvascular response to adenosine. In particular, previous studies have found reduced response in patients with diabetes mellitus and hypertension (52, 53), whereas we did not find such an association in our cohort."
  - "Furthermore, there are studies that indicate an association between plaque characteristics and the downstream microvascular response, which is independent of the degree of stenosis (54, 55). Unfortunately, data on plaque morphology were not available in the present study."
  - "Stable CAD represents a heterogeneous group of patients with a high variety of obstructive disease and microvascular response. Moreover, the skewed number of females versus men, and in particular, the low number of female patients was a hindrance to performing individual feature analysis stratified by sex. Studies in much larger populations are needed to fully analyze the association between focal disease, microvascular response, and patient and lesion characteristics."
  - "Previous studies have shown that a combination of angiography-derived anatomy and pressure data with computational fluid dynamics can reliably estimate flow and resistance (56). However, errors in segmented geometry, combined with the optimization process used to align with invasive pressure data, can affect the estimation of derived flow and resistance metrics."
  - "The right part of Fig. 5 shows the derived CFR versus invasive FFR, which is qualitatively similar to previous comparisons of invasive CFR versus FFR (2). However, although a modest but significant linear relationship between CFR and FFR was found in Ref. 2, we did not find a significant linear relationship in our data. This could be related to the exclusion of lesions with low resting ΔP (Pd/Pa > 0.99) in our analysis, which can be reflected in the underrepresentation of healthy cases (i.e., high FFR) in the right part of Fig. 5 compared with the data reported in Ref. 2."
  - "Nevertheless, future validation studies should focus on the comparison of CCTA-derived (changes in) flow and resistance with other noninvasive (positron emission tomography) and invasive approaches (invasive Doppler ultrasound and thermodilution) for quantifying flow and resistance."
  - Also from Discussion, on unexplained scatter: "variability in microvascular response to adenosine (see Fig. 5) and sensitivity analysis (see Fig. 4) suggest that additional factors are required to explain differences in adenosine response in symptomatic patients with stable CAD," and "microvascular dysfunction is a highly prevalent and heterogeneous disease that is difficult to diagnose (47), which might also explain the lack of improvement in overall diagnostic performance from our simplified model for coronary autoregulation."
  - Also, on data exclusion: "Lesions with a Pd/Pa greater than 0.99 were excluded from this analysis, as estimated resistance from low ΔP and/or low flow can be ill-defined (24)."
- Limitations I can see that they do NOT admit: (1) No per-case reclassification table is given, so the reader cannot see how many of the 280 lesions actually crossed the FFR ≤0.80 threshold between the two models (only aggregate sensitivity/specificity shifts are reported, from which threshold-crossing must be inferred, not confirmed). (2) The autoregulation model's key input, x = 1 − Pd/Pa at the outlet, is itself computed inside the same simulation/optimization pipeline that the authors say is vulnerable to segmentation error ("errors in segmented geometry ... can affect the estimation of derived flow and resistance metrics") — i.e., the coupling variable used to justify the model is not independent of the geometry-error risk the paper itself flags. (3) The decay constant b was fixed to 4 rather than estimated with uncertainty quantification, and "an initial optimization procedure" for choosing it is not described in enough detail to audit for overfitting. (4) No segmentation reproducibility/inter-observer analysis is performed at all (contrast with e.g. Donnelly et al. 2018), despite the pipeline depending on manually corrected lumen segmentation.

- 3–6 unresolved questions this paper leaves open, each one sentence:
  1. Whether a multivariate (rather than univariate F-score) model of patient/lesion features would reveal further, currently masked, determinants of the microvascular response, since the sample size "prevent[ed] a proper multivariate analysis."
  2. Whether incorporating plaque morphology data (unavailable here) would explain additional variance in the downstream microvascular response independent of stenosis degree.
  3. Whether the large residual scatter in kinvasive vs. Pd/Pa (Fig. 5) — explicitly stated as "cannot be explained by autoregulation mechanisms" — reflects a distinct microvascular-dysfunction phenotype that a more complex model could capture.
  4. Whether errors in segmented geometry meaningfully bias the derived kinvasive/resistance estimates, a concern the authors raise but do not quantify ("errors in segmented geometry, combined with the optimization process used to align with invasive pressure data, can affect the estimation of derived flow and resistance metrics").
  5. Whether the sex- and LAD/non-LAD-specific differences in microvascular response would replicate in a larger, better sex-balanced cohort.
  6. Whether direct, independent measurement of flow/resistance (PET, Doppler, thermodilution) would validate the CCTA+CFD-derived flow and resistance estimates used throughout this study.

## T6 decisive questions

**1. Exactly how is the microvascular/outlet resistance model formulated, and how does it depend on stenosis severity or distal pressure?**

Outlet resistance is computed from a resting-state simulation, then scaled down for hyperemia:
- Resting resistance: "Rrest_out,l = (Prest_out,l − Pv)/Qrest_out,l, with l = 1,...,Nout" (Eq. 2), Pv = 5 mmHg fixed.
- Hyperemic resistance: "Rhyp_out,l = Rrest_out,l / k, with l = 1,...,Nout" (Eq. 3).
- Two models for k are compared. Fixed-response: "kfixed = c" (Eq. 6), a single population constant. Autoregulation-aware: "kautoregulation = 1 + (khealthy − 1) · e^{bx}, x = 1 − Pd/Pa(rest,out,l)" (Eq. 7), where khealthy is "the microvascular response before any exhaustion due to autoregulation (i.e., for vessels without any pressure loss Pd/Pa = 1 and x = 0 ... kautoregulation = khealthy)," and b (fixed at 4) "controls how the microvascular response drops for increasing severity of the disease." The paper states the intended behavior is decay: "the reduction of the resistance from rest to hyperemia was designed to exponentially decay for increasing pressure loss in the resting condition" (i.e., k falls toward 1 as the resting pressure drop across the stenosis, x, grows — a direct, explicit functional dependence of the hyperemic resistance-reduction factor on stenosis-induced pressure loss, not merely on anatomy in isolation).

**2. Evidence that the adenosine vasodilatory response varies with lesion severity.**

From invasively derived kinvasive (Eq. 1, matched to measured Pd/Pa and FFR): "we found a significant correlation (P value 0.008) of this parameter with the severity of the epicardial stenosis" (Discussion). This corresponds to the feature-analysis result in Table 6: Pd/Pa F-statistic = 7.231, P = 0.008 for association with kinvasive. Model-fit evidence: incorporating this dependence (CT-FFRautoregulation) raised R² from 0.179 to 0.398 and reduced SD from 0.1167 to 0.1006 (P = 0.013) relative to the fixed model (Table 2). Figure 5 (left) "shows Eq. 7 fitted to the data, which indicates a general decline in the derived microvascular response to adenosine for increasing stenosis severity," while noting "a large amount of scatter ... indicating a large variability in the response, which cannot be explained by autoregulation mechanisms."

**3. Would outlet resistance change automatically if geometry were altered, or would it need separate re-tuning?**

Partially automatic, partially not. Automatic component: Rrest_out,l is recomputed from Prest_out,l, the pressure obtained by simulating the (possibly altered) geometry with a prescribed outflow (step 2 of the pipeline: "perform a resting-state simulation with prescribed inlet pressure Pinlet and prescribed outlet flows ... to find Prest_out,l"). Because a geometry change (e.g., a more severe stenosis, or a removed branch that redistributes flow/pressure) changes the simulated pressure field, Rrest_out,l changes as a direct simulation consequence — no manual step is needed for that part. Under the autoregulation model, this pressure change also propagates into x = 1 − Pd/Pa(rest,out,l), so kautoregulation (and hence Rhyp_out,l) shifts automatically with the new resting Pd/Pa at that outlet — the model is explicitly "designed" to respond this way. Non-automatic component: the mapping constants that translate x into k — khealthy (autoregulation model) or c (fixed model) — are population-level values obtained by "a 10-fold randomized cross-validation experiment where we optimized the value of c ... and khealthy ... by maximizing the coefficient of determination (R²score) between the predicted and invasive FFR" against the original cohort; they are not re-derived per geometry instance and the paper gives no mechanism for updating them when geometry is edited. So: the resistance level tracks the corrupted geometry automatically through the pressure-drop channel, but the calibration of how strongly it should track (khealthy, b, c) is frozen from the original fit and would need to be treated deliberately (kept fixed, or explicitly re-optimized) by any protocol that perturbs geometry — the paper does not test or endorse either choice.

**4. Why did AUC stay unchanged while R² improved? What does that imply about BC adaptation compensating for upstream error?**

Authors' own explanation (Discussion): "The improvements in standard deviation and limits of agreement were mainly driven by better agreement in predictions in moderate to severe lesions, whereas predictions in low-grade stenoses were less sensitive to the way hyperemia was modeled. This is natural because the behavior of the two models is similar in low-grade stenosis cases. The latter was also made evident from our Sobol sensitivity analysis, which showed that the applied model for autoregulation only played a secondary role in lesions with CT-FFR values greater than 0.7. However, because CT-FFR models omit autoregulatory mechanisms, optimization can yield an unfavorable sensitivity-specificity trade-off, as the fit is pushed to maximize agreement metrics (e.g., the variance explained, standard deviation, etc.) in addition to diagnostic performance. Hence, despite providing similar AUC, the reduced variance, the improved R2 score, and a better trade-off between sensitivity and specificity in CT-FFRautoregulation ultimately improve the overall credibility of the model compared with CT-FFRfixed."

Implication for T6: this is a directly relevant dissociation. Improving/changing the outlet BC model left the *aggregate, rank-based* discrimination metric (AUC 0.845 vs. 0.845, P = 0.915) completely flat, yet it produced a statistically significant shift in *threshold-based* classification performance — sensitivity rose from 58.1% to 68.6% (P = 0.0033) while specificity fell (90.3%→87.4%, not significant) — meaning individual lesions did cross the FFR ≤0.80 decision boundary between the two BC models even though the summary AUC number did not move. In other words, a BC-model change here demonstrably altered which side of the clinical decision threshold individual cases fell on, while the headline discrimination statistic (AUC) stayed silent about it — exactly the kind of masking T6 is testing for, though here it arises from choosing a better BC model, not from re-tuning a corrupted geometry to hit a validation target.

**7. DECISIVE JUDGEMENT.**

See final assistant message below (also reproduced here for completeness).

Design constraints a re-tuning protocol must satisfy to remain physiologically defensible, each tied to a quote:

1. **Outlet resistance is not a free scalar — it must be coupled to the resting pressure drop (a stenosis-severity proxy), not set independently.** "the reduction of the resistance from rest to hyperemia was designed to exponentially decay for increasing pressure loss in the resting condition" (Eq. 7 description). A protocol that re-tunes k (or Rhyp) to hit an outlet target without regard to the outlet's own resting Pd/Pa reproduces exactly the "fixed-response" model this paper shows is the physiologically weaker option (R² 0.179 vs. 0.398).

2. **Any re-tuning after a geometry edit must re-derive the resting-state pressure field first, since the coupling variable (x) is pressure-drop-, not geometry-label-, based.** The pipeline requires "perform a resting-state simulation with prescribed inlet pressure Pinlet and prescribed outlet flows ... to find Prest_out,l" (step 2) before Rrest_out,l (Eq. 2) and x = 1 − Pd/Pa(rest,out,l) (Eq. 7) can be computed. Re-tuning that skips recomputation of the resting state after a geometry perturbation (e.g., only re-fits the hyperemic BC to a flow/pressure target) breaks the very mechanism this paper uses to justify physiological coupling.

3. **Population-level calibration constants (khealthy, b, c) must not be freely re-fit per corrupted case, because the authors' own methodology treats free per-case fitting as unreliable.** "The decay-factor b (in Eq. 7) was fixed to a value of 4 during the optimization of CT-FFRautoregulation. Fixation of b resulted in more reliable predictions." If a re-tuning protocol allows every free parameter (including the shape of the resistance-severity relationship) to float per injected error, it forfeits the reliability basis this paper found necessary, and any "successful" fit to an outlet target says nothing about physiological correctness.

4. **A good aggregate/global fit does not certify correctness at the case level — large unexplained scatter persists even in the better model.** "a large amount of scatter is present, indicating a large variability in the response, which cannot be explained by autoregulation mechanisms" (Fig. 5 legend). A re-tuning protocol that judges itself successful because it hits population-level agreement metrics (R², SD) cannot claim from that alone that any single corrupted-geometry case has a physiologically correct resistance — this paper's own best model leaves that gap open.

5. **Derived resistance/flow estimates from a geometry+pressure-optimization pipeline are themselves vulnerable to segmentation/geometry error, so injected errors will contaminate whatever "ground truth" outlet targets are used for validation.** "errors in segmented geometry, combined with the optimization process used to align with invasive pressure data, can affect the estimation of derived flow and resistance metrics." Any T6-style protocol that derives its outlet re-tuning targets from a pipeline of this type must treat the targets themselves as error-prone once geometry is deliberately corrupted, not as an independent ground truth.

**Does this paper already-refute the BC-absorption hypothesis, or merely constrain how it must be tested?**

It does not refute it — if anything, it supplies evidence consistent with partial absorption/masking, and it constrains, rather than forecloses, how the hypothesis must be tested. The paper's own headline result is precisely a dissociation between an aggregate/global discrimination metric and threshold-level classification: changing only the outlet BC model (fixed → autoregulation-aware, holding geometry and inlet conditions fixed) left AUC completely unchanged (0.845 vs. 0.845, P = 0.915) while producing a statistically significant swing in sensitivity (58.1%→68.6%, P = 0.0033) and a near-doubling of R² (0.179→0.398) — i.e., BC-model choice measurably moved individual cases across (or away from) the FFR ≤0.80 boundary and changed continuous-error calibration substantially, without moving the summary AUC number. That is direct empirical precedent for the specific failure mode T6 investigates (validation-target agreement masking classification-level change), even though it arises here from a BC *model* change rather than from re-tuning after geometry corruption specifically. What the paper constrains is the legitimacy conditions under which any BC re-tuning can be called physiological rather than an arbitrary knob-turn: resistance must be functionally tied to the (re-simulated) resting pressure drop at each outlet (constraint 1–2 above), the mapping from severity to resistance-reduction must come from population-calibrated, not per-case-free-fit, constants (constraint 3), aggregate-metric success must not be read as case-level correctness given persistent unexplained scatter (constraint 4), and the very geometry/pressure pipeline used to generate or validate re-tuned targets is itself acknowledged to be corrupted by segmentation error (constraint 5) — meaning a T6 protocol must independently justify its outlet targets rather than treating pipeline-internal agreement as proof of physiological fidelity.
