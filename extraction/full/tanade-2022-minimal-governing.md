## Problem & claim
- Imaging modality and domain: Coronary angiography (2D, ≥4 orthogonal views left / ≥2 right) used to reconstruct 3D→1D personalized coronary artery trees; 1D reduced-order CFD hemodynamic simulation of fractional flow reserve (FFR) in coronary artery disease.
- Task: Global uncertainty quantification / Sobol sensitivity analysis of a validated patient-specific 1D FFR CFD framework, used to design two "streamlined" (reduced-input) model variants and test whether they preserve diagnostic performance against invasive FFR.
- Stated gap the paper attacks: "As the focus has been on identifying sensitive inputs, it is unknown which parameters are insensitive to FFR and could be relegated to patient averages, or patient-generalized parameters, without sacrificing diagnostic performance." Prior UQ/SA studies "rely on models that have not been validated against FFRinvasive measurements and are limited by small cohort sizes."
- Central claim in one sentence: In a validated 50-patient 1D FFR CFD model, only cardiac output and stenosis degree (plus, to a lesser but still-significant extent, distal pressure-sampling location) needed to be patient-tuned — mean arterial pressure, heart rate, hematocrit, and inlet flow-rate waveform could be patient-generalized (population-average) — while personalized coronary geometry was retained as an input to every model variant and was asserted, not sensitivity-tested, to be the most important requirement.

## Method
- Architecture / algorithm: (1) 3D coronary tree reconstructed semi-automatically from paired angiograms (Chen & Carroll algorithm), validated topologically/anatomically by an expert interventional cardiologist against ImageJ-measured minimal luminal diameters on a third angiographic view. (2) 1D centerlines + hydraulic diameters extracted (Mimics) to build a 1D full coronary tree geometry — quasi-rigid-walled 1D blood flow solver (mass/momentum/pressure-area constitutive equations, MacCormack finite-difference scheme), with an explicit stenosis pressure-loss term (Eq. 5, Young/Kv,Kt,Ku-parameterized) coupled at the stenosis location. (3) Inlet: pulsatile flow-rate waveform (Hadjiloizou-derived, patient-scaled by cardiac output/heart rate/flow dominance; hyperemia scaled 4x LCA / 3x RCA). (4) Outlets: 2-element Windkessel (Rp, C) per terminal vessel; C fixed at 9 cm⁴s²g⁻¹ (population constant); Rp distributed across terminal branches via a resistance–radius (Murray's-law-type) relationship, Eq. 8: Ri = Pmean·(Σrj³)/(Qostial·ri³), scaled by 0.22× for hyperemia. (5) Global variance-based Sobol sensitivity analysis (Eck et al. method) via second-order Saltelli sampling over 4 scalar clinical inputs, >1,000,000 simulations, run for 2 weeks on 32 compute nodes.
- Key technical novelty vs. closest prior work: A Sobol/Saltelli global UQ analysis performed on a CFD FFR model that had itself first been validated against invasive FFR (unlike Fossan et al. 2018, Morris et al. 2017, Sankaran et al. 2016, which the authors say relied on unvalidated or 3D-ground-truth models), in a larger cohort (N=50) than prior UQ studies.
- Loss functions / training regime: N/A — not a trained/learned model; a mechanistic 1D CFD solver with sampled input perturbations.
- Inputs/outputs, dimensionality: Inputs — per-patient reconstructed 1D coronary geometry (vessel centerlines/hydraulic diameters at 100 μm resolution), stenosis length/minimal luminal radius, mean arterial pressure, cardiac output, heart rate, hematocrit, distal pressure-sampling location (5–65 mm). Outputs — time-averaged pressure at the distal location over the last of 20 simulated cardiac cycles (grid spacing 500 μm, timestep 1e-5 s), converted to FFR (ratio to aortic pressure); classification threshold FFR ≤ 0.80 = ischemic.

## Data
- Datasets: N=50 patients, retrospective single-center cohort (Brigham and Women's Hospital, Boston), angiographically-documented CAD, Jan 1 2016–Aug 1 2018, invasive FFR measured for at least one vessel per patient. Exclusions: prior CABG, STEMI, chronic total occlusion, ostial lesions. Private clinical cohort; no public release; IRB: Massachusetts General Brigham #2015P001084; no individual informed consent required (retrospective, no patient interaction).
- Annotation protocol: 3D reconstructions validated by "an expert interventional cardiologist" comparing minimal luminal diameter in the 3D model vs. a third (independent) angiographic view. Stenosis anatomical position and degree labeled by "expert interventional cardiologists." A single researcher-vs-cardiologist stenosis-degree comparison (blinded to each other) was used only to derive the worst-case *uncertainty bound* for stenosis degree in Table 1 (SD = 16.9%), not as a full inter-rater agreement study, and not applied to geometry/topology. Distal pressure-sampling location chosen by "an experienced interventional cardiologist" (5–65 mm range); CV=0.117 estimated from the cohort for its uncertainty bound.
- Train/val/test split: Not an ML train/test study. No external validation cohort. FFRbaseline, FFRsemi-streamlined, FFRstreamlined are all evaluated on the same 50-patient cohort; researchers performing reconstructions/CFD were blinded to invasive FFR until validation was complete.

## Evaluation
- Metrics, headline results:
  - FFRbaseline vs. FFRinvasive: r = 0.714 (abstract) / 0.71 (in-text), p < 0.001; mean difference 0.01 ± 0.09; sensitivity 89.5% (95% CI 66.9–98.7), specificity 93.6% (95% CI 78.6–99.2), PPV 89.5%, NPV 93.6%, accuracy 92.0%, AUC 0.95; ROC-recovered ischemic threshold 0.79–0.80.
  - FFRsemi-streamlined vs. FFRbaseline: r = 0.96, mean diff 0.00 ± 0.04. vs. FFRinvasive: r = 0.75, mean diff 0.01 ± 0.08; sensitivity/specificity/PPV/NPV/accuracy "identical to FFRbaseline" (89.5/93.6/89.5/93.6/92.0%); AUC 0.96; threshold 0.79–0.81.
  - FFRstreamlined vs. FFRbaseline: r = 0.84, mean diff 0.01 ± 0.07. vs. FFRinvasive: r = 0.64, p < 0.001, mean diff 0.01 ± 0.08; sensitivity 79.0% (54.4–94.0), specificity 90.3% (74.3–98.0), PPV 83.3%, NPV 87.5%, accuracy 86.0%; AUC 0.90; threshold 0.78–0.80. Average % discrepancy vs. FFRbaseline: 3.3% (semi-streamlined), 5.7% (streamlined).
  - Model comparison statistics: paired Wilcoxon signed-rank with Holm-Bonferroni correction; "not statistically significant for FFRstreamlined vs. FFRbaseline (p = 0.125)" and "diagnostic metrics were identical between FFRsemistreamlined vs. FFRbaseline."
  - Sobol sensitivity (Figure 4; exact index values NOT REPORTED in text, only qualitative ranking and the 0.05 significance threshold): "cardiac output and stenosis degree contributed most to the variance in FFR. Distal location and mean arterial pressure also exceeded the threshold for sensitivity, but contributed less to the variance in FFR than cardiac output and stenosis degree." "Total and main effects were not statistically different, which meant that interaction effects between input parameters were insignificant" (i.e., Si ≈ STi).
  - Reclassification-vs-uncertainty (Section 3.5/Figure 7, resampling CO and stenosis degree only, distal location and MAP fixed at patient baseline): "At one standard deviation, 50% of cases were reclassified in the RCA and 25% of cases were reclassified in the LCA."
  - Subgroup ANOVA: grey-zone (p=0.0356), anatomy (p=0.0357), repeated measures (p<0.0001) significant main effects; anatomy×repeated-measures interaction p=0.0293; anatomy main effect significant for cardiac output (p=0.0279), stenosis degree (p=0.0025), mean arterial pressure (p=0.0266); distal location "no significant effects." Post hoc: cardiac-output uncertainty larger effect on LCA than RCA (p<0.05); stenosis-degree uncertainty larger effect on RCA than LCA (p<0.001).
- Baselines compared against: FFRinvasive (gold standard); FFRbaseline as the internal high-cost reference for the two streamlined variants; brief external comparison of computation time/accuracy vs. Siemens cFFR and Toshiba CT-FFR 1D models (23.9±11.2 min and 27.1±7.5 min vs. this study's 10.1±4.7 min).
- Statistical testing: Kolmogorov-Smirnov (central limit theorem check), least-squares correlation, Bland-Altman, ROC/AUC, paired t-test / Wilcoxon signed-rank with Holm-Bonferroni correction, global repeated-measures ANOVA with post hoc two-tailed t-tests, bootstrapped 95% CIs for Sobol convergence (width <10% of max index).
- Compute reported: >1,000,000 simulations, "wall clock duration of two weeks on 32 compute nodes," embarrassingly parallel scheme; average per-case 1D framework computation time 10.1 ± 4.7 min. No GPU/param counts (not a learned model).

## Limitations
- Limitations the authors admit (Section 4.5, verbatim): "Regarding limitations, the patient population was retrospective and from a single center. A prospective study from multiple centers would provide a more robust validation, but the point of this work was to develop an optimized model from a fully patient-specific model, and validating with clinically measured FFR demonstrated that both models were accurate. Second, the correlation between FFRbaseline and FFRinvasive was moderate. There are multiple ways to validate FFRbaseline against the clinical ground-truth... While the Bland-Altman mean differences indicated negligible bias, the correlation was moderate... Third, the effect of adenosine was considered generalizable across the cohort. Lo et al. (83) recently compared patient-specific outflow conditions based on myocardial perfusion from positron emission tomography data to the conventional scaling method. They found that the effect of adenosine could be overestimated and result in overestimating FFR severity. The FFRbaseline vs. FFRinvasive slope exceeded unity, which could indicate that our model overestimated hyperemia because of using scaling laws. This could be rectified in future works by acquiring myocardial perfusion data and tuning hyperemia on a per-patient level (83, 84). Fourth, the factorial study subdividing patient-specificity by anatomic and hemodynamic classes was limited. Although we explored coronary anatomy, ischemic vs. non-ischemic stenoses, and grey-zone cases, the study could have also explored differing effects of patient-specificity on factors such as age, sex, and the presence of co-morbidities. A larger cohort would be required to expand on the number of factors investigated. We also primarily focused on focal lesions. Including complex coronary disease, such as ostial and bifurcation stenoses, would require separate sensitivity studies as we expect hemodynamics to differ from focal stenoses. For example, ostial stenoses may have an impact on inlet coronary waveforms and bifurcation stenoses may increase the importance of segmenting accurate stenoses."
- Limitations I can see that they do NOT admit: (1) Geometry/topology is never a perturbed variable in the Sobol analysis, so the paper's own headline claim ("Capturing coronary hemodynamics depended most on accurate geometry reconstruction") is not backed by a Sobol index for geometry — it is an assertion built into the study design (geometry always held at its "accurate," expert-validated value), not a tested finding. (2) "Stenosis degree" (a single scalar, Eq. 6) is used as the entire proxy for stenosis/geometric uncertainty; no eccentricity, stenosis-length independent perturbation, or side-branch/topological error is ever introduced. (3) Windkessel peripheral resistance (Eq. 8) is always computed from the patient's own reconstructed terminal-branch radii in all three model variants (baseline/semi-streamlined/streamlined) — "generalization" in this paper never means replacing anatomy-informed resistance distribution with a population-generic value; it only means generalizing the scalar physiological terms (mean arterial pressure, distal location) that scale that per-patient, geometry-derived resistance distribution. (4) No re-tuning-after-geometry-change experiment of any kind is performed. (5) The authors themselves flag, without exploring, that "bifurcation stenoses may increase the importance of segmenting accurate stenoses" — i.e., they explicitly did not test topologically complex (side-branch/bifurcation) segmentation error, which is exactly the T6 error class.

## Openings
- Explicit "future work" statements, quoted: "This could be rectified in future works by acquiring myocardial perfusion data and tuning hyperemia on a per-patient level (83, 84)." "A larger cohort would be required to expand on the number of factors investigated." "Including complex coronary disease, such as ostial and bifurcation stenoses, would require separate sensitivity studies as we expect hemodynamics to differ from focal stenoses... bifurcation stenoses may increase the importance of segmenting accurate stenoses."
- Unresolved questions this paper leaves open:
  1. Whether Sobol sensitivity rankings would change if coronary geometry/topology itself (not just a scalar "stenosis degree") were perturbed — never tested here.
  2. Whether bifurcation/side-branch/ostial lesions, explicitly excluded ("primarily focused on focal lesions"), would show different (the authors suggest greater) sensitivity to segmentation accuracy.
  3. Whether generalized peripheral-resistance distribution (as opposed to the geometry-derived per-patient distribution always used here) would preserve diagnostic performance — not tested, since Rp apportionment is always anatomy-derived in every model variant presented.
  4. Whether outlet boundary conditions re-tuned after a geometric change could mask or compensate for that geometric error — no such experiment appears anywhere in the paper.
  5. Whether the observed anatomy-dependence (LCA vs. RCA differing sensitivity to cardiac output and stenosis degree) generalizes to a multi-center, prospective cohort.

## T6 decisive questions

**1. Exactly what parameters entered the Sobol sensitivity analysis, and exactly what was held fixed? Was GEOMETRY itself a varied parameter, or only physiological/BC parameters on fixed geometries?**
Only four scalar clinical inputs entered the Saltelli/Sobol sampling: "mean arterial pressure, cardiac output, stenosis degree, and distal location" (Table 1: distal location N(30.0, 3.5) mm; cardiac output factor N(1, 0.153); stenosis degree addition N(0, 16.9%); mean arterial pressure factor N(1, 0.056)). The paper is explicit that this was a deliberate narrowing from a larger raw-input list: "The raw inputs to define personalized blood flow simulations included mean arterial pressure, cardiac output, coronary geometry, heart rate, stenosis anatomy, hematocrit, and distal location. We considered stenosis anatomy separately from coronary geometry... To prevent diluting the parameter space with insignificant parameters, the raw inputs were narrowed to mean arterial pressure, cardiac output, stenosis degree, and distal location." Held fixed/never perturbed: **coronary geometry itself** (the reconstructed 1D tree — topology, vessel radii, lengths), heart rate, hematocrit, vessel wall properties (h, E, ν), and Windkessel compliance C (fixed population constant, 9 cm⁴s²g⁻¹). "Stenosis anatomy" was reduced to a single scalar, stenosis degree, not full stenosis-length/eccentricity/shape variation, and not topology. Geometry was **never a varied Sobol parameter** — it was held at its per-patient, expert-validated value in every simulation of the uncertainty analysis.

**2. What is the precise evidence for "geometry dominates"? Give the Sobol indices or equivalent numbers.**
There is no Sobol index for geometry — none exists, because geometry was never perturbed. The exact numeric Sobol indices for the four tested parameters are NOT REPORTED in the text (they appear only in Figure 4, which is a bar chart, not transcribable from pdftotext); the text gives only a qualitative ranking and the significance threshold: "cardiac output and stenosis degree contributed most to the variance in FFR. Distal location and mean arterial pressure also exceeded the threshold for sensitivity, but contributed less to the variance in FFR... Sobol indices exceeding a threshold of 0.05 were considered significant." The "geometry dominates" language is an assertion, not a measured Sobol result: "Coronary anatomy was used to define the computational domain and was required to be reconstructed accurately on a per-patient level" (Methods, stated as a design requirement, not a tested sensitivity), and the abstract conclusion — "Capturing coronary hemodynamics depended most on accurate geometry reconstruction and cardiac output measurement" — bundles an untested factor (geometry) with a tested one (cardiac output) without distinguishing that only the latter has a Sobol index behind it.

**3. What exactly do "baseline", "semi-streamlined" and "streamlined" models differ in? Which used patient-tuned Windkessel resistances and which used generalized ones?**
FFRbaseline: full patient-tuned inputs — geometry, stenosis geometry, mean arterial pressure, cardiac output, heart rate, hematocrit, distal pressure location, all patient-specific. FFRsemi-streamlined: "incorporated patient-generalized mean arterial pressure, and patient-specific cardiac output, stenosis degree, and distal location" (i.e., only MAP generalized to the cohort average, 87.3 mmHg). FFRstreamlined: "used patient-generalized distal location and mean arterial pressure, and patient-specific cardiac output and stenosis degree" (distal location generalized to 30 mm as well as MAP; heart rate and hematocrit also generalized in Table 4, 70.8 bpm and 39.2%). Critically, **none of the three variants uses a topology-blind "generalized" Windkessel resistance value.** Peripheral resistance in all three is computed from the same per-patient formula, Eq. 8 — "Ri = Pmean·Σrj³/(Qostial·ri³)" — using each patient's own reconstructed terminal-branch radii (ri, rj), which are never generalized in any variant. What is generalized between variants is only the Pmean and (indirectly) Qostial/CO terms that scale this geometry-derived resistance distribution: "Patient-generalized cardiac output (4.5 L/min) was used to derive inlet flow waveforms, but patient-specific cardiac output was used to compute peripheral resistance." So resistance apportionment across the tree is always anatomy-informed; only its overall scale (via MAP) is generalized in the semi-streamlined and streamlined variants.

**4. Does the paper anywhere RE-TUNE boundary conditions after changing geometry? Quote it, or state plainly that it does not.**
It does not. No passage in the paper describes perturbing/changing coronary geometry and then re-deriving or re-tuning outlet boundary conditions (Windkessel resistances or otherwise) to compensate. Geometry is reconstructed once per patient, validated by an expert cardiologist, and used as a fixed input across FFRbaseline, FFRsemi-streamlined, and FFRstreamlined alike; the only "streamlining" performed is on scalar physiological inputs (MAP, distal location, heart rate, hematocrit, inlet-flow generalization), never on geometry, and there is no re-tuning-after-geometry-change experiment anywhere in Methods, Results, or Discussion.

**5. Does it report per-case FFR reclassification across 0.80 (how many cases flipped between model variants), or only aggregate sensitivity/specificity/AUC? Quote the exact numbers.**
Both exist but answer different questions. (a) Model-variant comparison (baseline vs. semi-streamlined vs. streamlined) is reported ONLY as aggregate metrics (Table 5): e.g., sensitivity 79.0% (streamlined) vs. 89.5% (baseline/semi-streamlined), and via a paired Wilcoxon test on those aggregate metrics ("not statistically significant for FFRstreamlined vs. FFRbaseline (p = 0.125)"). No per-case count of how many of the 50 patients flipped classification between FFRbaseline and FFRstreamlined is given anywhere in the text. (b) A separate analysis — not a model-variant comparison, but a resampling of the *uncertainty* in cardiac output and stenosis degree — does report a per-case reclassification proportion: "At one standard deviation, 50% of cases were reclassified in the RCA and 25% of cases were reclassified in the LCA." This reclassification is driven by resampling within-patient physiological input uncertainty (CO, stenosis degree) at increasing standard deviations, not by comparing model architectures, and never involves a geometric/topological change.

**6. Its own stated limitations, verbatim.**
Quoted in full above under "Limitations" (Section 4.5). In brief: (i) retrospective, single-center cohort; (ii) moderate correlation between FFRbaseline and FFRinvasive; (iii) adenosine/hyperemia effect assumed generalizable across the cohort, and the FFRbaseline-vs-FFRinvasive slope exceeding unity may indicate hyperemia overestimation from scaling laws; (iv) the anatomic/hemodynamic factorial subdivision was limited (didn't explore age, sex, comorbidities) and the study "primarily focused on focal lesions," explicitly excluding ostial and bifurcation stenoses, which the authors say "would require separate sensitivity studies... bifurcation stenoses may increase the importance of segmenting accurate stenoses."

**7. DECISIVE JUDGEMENT: does this paper already demonstrate, or already refute, the claim that BC re-tuning absorbs topological segmentation error?**
**NEITHER-ADDRESSES-IT.** The Sobol sensitivity analysis in this paper perturbs only four scalar physiological/BC-scaling inputs (cardiac output, distal location, stenosis degree, mean arterial pressure) on a single, fixed, expert-validated coronary geometry per patient — geometry/topology is never itself a varied parameter, so the paper's "geometry dominates" language (abstract) is an unmeasured assertion, not a Sobol-index-backed finding, and cannot be read as evidence that geometric/topological error would dominate over BC re-tuning in a controlled comparison. Separately, none of the three model variants (baseline/semi-streamlined/streamlined) ever uses a topology-blind "generalized" Windkessel resistance — peripheral resistance in all three is always computed from each patient's own reconstructed terminal-branch radii (Eq. 8), so the paper never even constructs the comparison T6 needs (a truly generic, anatomy-blind outlet BC) let alone tests whether re-tuning it after a geometric perturbation could mask that perturbation. Most decisively, there is no passage anywhere in the paper describing a geometry change followed by BC re-tuning — the experiment T6 is designed around simply does not occur here. The authors' own limitations section even flags, without investigating, that bifurcation/side-branch topology (T6's exact error class) "may increase the importance of segmenting accurate stenoses," effectively conceding the question is open. This paper should therefore be de-escalated from "threat" to "not applicable/orthogonal": it supports a narrower, uncontested point (some scalar physiological measurements can be safely generalized on a *fixed, accurate* geometry) but says nothing, for or against, about whether BC re-tuning can absorb *topological* segmentation error.
