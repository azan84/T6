## Problem & claim
- Imaging modality and domain: Coronary CT angiography (CTA)-derived patient-specific 3D coronary artery models; downstream quantity of interest is FFRCT (CT-derived fractional flow reserve). Training/test cohorts drawn from the DiscoverFlow (90 patients) and DeFACTO (240 patients) multicenter clinical trials.
- Task: Other — forward geometric uncertainty quantification (UQ) through a 3D CFD (Navier–Stokes/FFRCT) pipeline, accelerated by a machine-learning surrogate (decision-tree regressor) trained to approximate simulation-based FFRCT, combined with an adaptive stochastic collocation method to propagate geometric perturbations.
- Stated gap the paper attacks (quoted, Section 1): "a comprehensive assessment of diverse sources of uncertainties including clinical and geometrical variables on patient-specific models has not been performed to-date owing to the following challenges — (a) depending on the size of the arterial tree, it is computationally intractable to quantify the impact of a large number of uncertain variables, and (b) parameterizing and defining shape-space for patient-specific geometries has not yet been performed." Also: "Solving stochastic differential equations under geometric uncertainty has not been explored for complex and/or patient-specific geometries. This is the first time uncertainty with respect to patient-specific vascular geometry is performed."
- Central claim in one sentence: The paper proposes a segment-wise stochastic parameterization of lumen-area geometric uncertainty, propagated via adaptive Smolyak stochastic collocation and accelerated with a bootstrap-aggregated decision-tree machine-learning surrogate (trained on DiscoverFlow/DeFACTO data), showing that the ML surrogate reproduces 3D-simulation FFRCT sensitivity to geometric perturbation with correlation 0.92, and that diseased large-artery segments, terminal segments (dilation-sensitive) and proximal healthy/critical-transport segments (erosion-sensitive) are the most sensitive to geometric uncertainty.
- Explicit novelty claims (Section 1, quoted): "Novel developments include (a) a subdivision strategy to define a stochastic space of patient-specific geometries, (b) a machine learning algorithm to accelerate solution to Navier–Stokes equations using a large database of patient-specific solutions, and (c) a reduced order model used as a feature in the machine learning algorithm that simplifies the solution to the Navier–Stokes equations by solving flowrate and pressure loss only using lumen areas."

## Method
- Governing equations: Incompressible Navier–Stokes (Eq. 1), rigid walls, Newtonian fluid, µ = 0.04 g/cm·s, ρ = 1.06 g/cm³; stabilized finite element (generalized-α method), linear basis elements. Dirichlet velocity inlet at aorta; outlets modeled with a "resistance condition which couples blood pressure and velocity at outlet nodes" (Section 2.2). FFRCT(x) = Pc(x)/Paorta.
- Geometry representation (Section 2.3): lumen area A(.) sampled at NCL centerline points, grouped into NS segments Si bounded by branch points/inlet/outlet (Eqs. 2–3). "N S and NCL are patient-specific and can vary widely across patients."
- Perturbation model (Section 2.3, Eqs. 4–5): perturbation of segment i's area, ∆A(Si, ξ) ≡ α(ck,i, ci,o, σ; ξ), where α is either
  - a spatial Gaussian centered on the segment midpoint: α(ck,i, ci,o, σ; ξ) = u0,i(ξ) · (1/√(2πσi)) · exp(−d(ck,i,ci,o)²/2σi²), with correlation length σ set to 1/6 of the segment length "so that the segment ends are at a distance 3σ from center with negligible perturbation" — used "to validate the model by comparing sensitivities" (i.e., the 3D-simulation validation arm), or
  - a spatially uniform perturbation: α(ck,i, ci,o, σ; ξ) = u0,i(ξ) — "used ... to predict and report sensitivity values" (i.e., the production/ML arm), noted explicitly to violate C0 continuity at bifurcations: "The latter does not ensure C 0 continuity of the surface at bifurcation locations, and hence cannot be used with 3D simulations."
  - u0,i(ξ) is drawn as u0 ~ U[0, umax] (uniform case) or via inverse-erf for a Gaussian-distributed magnitude, "where umax is the maximum uncertainty defined usually as a percentage of the radius" (Section 2.4.1).
  - A surface-node extension is defined (Section 2.3) as ∆p(xi) = α(cxi, ci,o, σ; ξ)(xi − cxi), used "only for the validation problem to ensure continuity of the perturbed geometry" — i.e., this maps the same single per-segment scalar magnitude onto the 3D surface mesh, it does not introduce independent cross-sectional/eccentric degrees of freedom.
  - Explicit self-imposed scope limit (Section 1): "We limit the developments of this paper to uncertainty in lumen radius... The validation of the perturbation model is restricted to deformation maps with fixed bifurcation locations. Further, we assume rigid walls."
- Stochastic propagation: stochastic collocation (Xiu & Hesthaven; Babuska et al.) with the adaptive Smolyak sparse-grid algorithm (Section 2.4.1, Algorithm 1), 1D nested Chebyshev collocation nodes, frozen/active-point adaptive refinement.
- Machine-learning surrogate (Section 2.5–2.7): features = local/upstream/downstream radius, distance to nearest bifurcations, min/max/mean downstream outlet area, min upstream/downstream diameter, pressure recovery factor, a 15-component "health index"/disease-burden score (κ, γ) from three global kernel regressors (Appendix A), patient features (BSA, aortic/coronary flow, viscosity from hematocrit), and reduced-order-model features (net effective downstream resistance Reff, geometric resistance Rgeom via Poiseuille's law, three flow-rate estimates, Poiseuille/modified-Poiseuille pressure-drop estimates, pressure recovery factor; Appendix B). Regressor: bootstrap-aggregated (10 trees) decision tree, trained via information-gain (entropy) splitting (Section 2.6, Algorithm 2), computed in Weka.
- Sensitivity metric: σFFRCT = sqrt(Σ wi·FFRCTi² − µ²FFRCT), a quadrature-weighted standard deviation over collocation points (Section 3.3.2) — a continuous variance-type sensitivity index, not a classification/reclassification statistic.

- Geometric-parameterization robustness test (Section 3.2): "a patient-specific dataset" (singular), segment counts 55/150/300/600 (branch-based vs. centerline-aggregate splits of 20/10/5 points).

## Evaluation
- Mesh convergence (3.1.1): max FFRCT field error <2% at ~4M elements vs. ~40M-element reference, over 20 patients.
- Stochastic-space convergence (3.1.2): four-dimensional UQ test (aortic pressure, viscosity, and two flow-fraction parameters — myocardial mass and a scaling constant) comparing adaptive stochastic collocation, standard stochastic collocation, tensor-product grid, and Monte-Carlo (~600 runs as reference); relative-error statistic κ = |µ−µ*|/µ* + |σ−σ*|/σ* + |CI90−CI*90|/CI*90; adaptive method shown to need far fewer simulations for the same κ (Fig. 5); tolerance ϵ = 0.01.
- Geometric-parameterization robustness (3.2): segmental (branch-based) split (NS=55) used as the reported basis for the rest of the paper; "The sensitivity analysis based on branching location splits is an upper-bound on the sensitivity values obtained with finer spatial discretization" (up to NS=600).
- ML regressor accuracy vs. 3D-simulated FFRCT (Table 3, 3.3.1): decision tree (DT) trained on DiscoverFlow, tested on DeFACTO: ρ=0.909, MAE=0.031, RMS=0.049; DT trained on DeFACTO, tested on DiscoverFlow: ρ=0.943, MAE=0.026, RMS=0.040; decision-tree-with-linear-regression-leaves (DTR) variants: ρ=0.723/0.924. Intra-DeFACTO 158/82 split: ρ=0.943, MAE=0.026, RMS=0.040 (abstract's headline "correlation coefficient of training set was 0.94" refers to this).
- ML-vs-3D sensitivity comparison (3.3.2, Fig. 8): correlation 0.92 (over 20 patients, 30 segments, 20% perturbation magnitude), MAE=0.014, RMS=0.018.
- Compute/timing (Fig. 9): single 3D simulation "over a half hour on 90 cores"; ML surrogate "a few seconds on a single processor workstation." Full sensitivity calculation: 3D route "could take a few days even using 90 cores"; ML route "produces a sensitivity calculation in a few minutes" (elsewhere stated as "more than 5 days using 90 cores to 10 min in a single core machine," Fig. 9 caption).
- Geometric sensitivity results (3.4, Figs. 10–13): "Segments with significant disease in the larger arteries had the highest sensitivities" (Abstract); "Terminal segments are more sensitive to dilation and proximal healthy segments are more sensitive to erosion" (Abstract); asymmetric, non-linear sensitivity to dilation vs. erosion depending on whether geometric resistance is comparable to net downstream boundary resistance (Fig. 13, Section 4). Fig. 10 shows segments "ranked according to their worst case impact on FFRCT."
- Statistical testing/CIs: no p-values or hypothesis tests reported anywhere; results reported as correlation coefficients, MAE/RMS, and the σFFRCT standard-deviation sensitivity metric; CI90 used only as one of three convergence-diagnostic terms in Section 3.1.2, not reported per-patient or per-segment as an output.
- No confusion matrix, no reclassification count, no per-lesion/per-case binary threshold-crossing analysis is reported anywhere in the paper.

## Limitations
- Limitations the authors admit (Section 4, Discussion, quoted in full): "The main potential limitation of this approach is that the stochastic space of geometries does not encompass the true geometry. However, due to our conservative approach in defining the stochastic space of geometries (uniform random variable), the true geometry is not captured only in the case when the initial lumen segmentation has a large error from the true segmentation. To mitigate this error, in our geometric modeling process (which is a controlled manufacturing process regulated by the FDA), analysts go through sections of the model to ensure that the initial lumen segmentation is as accurate as possible against the image data. We also only captured uncertainty in the lumen area, since minimum lumen diameter is the most clinically relevant measure that is hypothesized to drive large pressure changes. However, uncertainty in surface profiles also needs to be investigated, along with the development of a geometry parameterization tool for surfaces (e.g. NURBS). The latter can also help define deformation maps at bifurcation locations for the 3D geometry. Future work could also include defining uncertainty based on local image quality. For example, locations of the coronary tree close to artifacts like blooming could have higher uncertainty. This can also be used to quantify if uncertainty in two regions are correlated. We also need to validate the sensitivities using clinical data. We can achieve this by calculating, say 95% confidence intervals and ensuring that 95% of measurement points lies within this. This, however, requires many patient-specific data and should be investigated in the future." Also (Section 1): "Further, we assume continuity of FFRCT as well as its derivatives in the stochastic space. The validation of the perturbation model is restricted to deformation maps with fixed bifurcation locations. Further, we assume rigid walls." Also (Section 3.4): "In general, sensitivity varies non-linearly with respect to magnitude of perturbation. A positive perturbation of a segment does not necessarily have the same impact magnitude as a negative perturbation."
- Limitations I can see that they do NOT admit: (1) The uncertainty magnitude umax is never sourced to any measured data of any kind (real inter-observer, inter-modality, or otherwise) anywhere in the text — it is simply "defined usually as a percentage of the radius," and the reported working values (20%, and 20/40/60% in Fig. 12) are not tied to any cited empirical segmentation-error study. (2) Geometric error is represented by exactly one scalar type per segment (a radial area dilation/erosion, applied either as a localized Gaussian bump or a uniform block) — there is no eccentricity/off-axis representation (perturbation is applied uniformly around the lumen circumference, only area/radius changes), no distinct stenosis-length error type, and no taper/undersizing error type distinguishable from plain dilation/erosion. (3) Bifurcation/branch topology is never perturbed and is explicitly held fixed by construction (correlation length forces zero perturbation at segment boundaries; the uniform-perturbation form is rejected for 3D use precisely because it would break continuity "at bifurcation locations"), so missed/spurious side-branch or connectivity error is categorically outside this paper's model. (4) All reported outcomes are continuous (σFFRCT, correlation coefficients, MAE/RMS, CI90 in the four-parameter convergence check only) — no per-case or per-lesion FFR reclassification across the 0.80 cutoff is ever computed, despite the 0.80 cutoff being invoked motivationally in the Introduction. (5) Boundary conditions (outlet resistances) are computed once from patient-specific clinical/anatomic data and never re-tuned or re-derived in response to a geometric perturbation; no tuned-vs-untuned BC comparison is attempted or discussed.

## Openings
- Explicit future-work statements (quoted, Section 4): "However, uncertainty in surface profiles also needs to be investigated, along with the development of a geometry parameterization tool for surfaces (e.g. NURBS). The latter can also help define deformation maps at bifurcation locations for the 3D geometry." "Future work could also include defining uncertainty based on local image quality. For example, locations of the coronary tree close to artifacts like blooming could have higher uncertainty. This can also be used to quantify if uncertainty in two regions are correlated." "We also need to validate the sensitivities using clinical data. We can achieve this by calculating, say 95% confidence intervals and ensuring that 95% of measurement points lies within this. This, however, requires many patient-specific data and should be investigated in the future." Also: "It would be interesting to study if the same features will be sufficient for performing machine learning on three-dimensional velocity fields or if additional features are required."
- 3–6 unresolved questions this paper leaves open, each one sentence:
  1. Whether the segment-uniform radial dilation/erosion perturbation, with no eccentricity, taper, or topological (missed side-branch) component, and no localized image-quality-driven spatial variation, materially understates real geometric uncertainty relative to actual lumen-segmentation error.
  2. Whether the assumed umax magnitude (a percentage of radius, never tied to a measured value) would change if replaced by a magnitude drawn from a genuine inter-observer or inter-modality segmentation-error study.
  3. Whether allowing deformation at bifurcation locations (explicitly excluded here: "validation of the perturbation model is restricted to deformation maps with fixed bifurcation locations") — including missed/spurious branches — would change the sensitivity ranking.
  4. Whether the continuous σFFRCT sensitivity metric, if converted into a per-lesion/per-case binary reclassification rate across the 0.80 FFRCT cutoff, would show clinically different (more actionable) results than the reported standard-deviation/correlation statistics.
  5. Whether re-tuning outlet boundary resistances after a geometric perturbation (rather than holding them fixed from patient clinical data) would absorb or mask the geometric sensitivity found here — a comparison never attempted in this paper.
  6. Whether "validate the sensitivities using clinical data" (explicitly named as required future work) would use real inter-observer segmentation data, and whether that would confirm or overturn the segment-ranking conclusions.

## T6 decisive questions

**1. Exactly how is geometric uncertainty parameterised? Quote the equations and the perturbation scheme. Is it one random variable per section, a spatially-varying random field, or something richer? Does it admit asymmetric, eccentric or localised shape error?**
Geometry is discretized into NS segments by branch/inlet/outlet locations (Eq. 3), and exactly ONE scalar random variable u0,i(ξ) per segment controls the perturbation magnitude of that segment's cross-sectional AREA. Two functional forms are given (Section 2.3): "The first is defined as a spatial Gaussian function centered on the center of the segment, α(ck,i, ci,o, σ; ξ) = u0,i(ξ)·(1/√2πσi)·e^(−d(ck,i,ci,o)²/2σi²) (Eq. 4) ... The other option is to define a uniform perturbation such that α(ck,i, ci,o, σ; ξ) = u0,i(ξ) (Eq. 5)." Both are governed by a single draw of u0,i per segment; the Gaussian form only varies the perturbation's magnitude smoothly along the segment's length (highest at the segment center, tapering to zero at segment ends) — it does not vary independently around the lumen circumference or introduce any off-axis/eccentric displacement. The extension to surface nodes (∆p(xi) = α(cxi,ci,o,σ;ξ)(xi − cxi)) reuses the same single per-segment magnitude, applied radially outward from the centerline at each surface point, so the cross-section dilates/erodes uniformly (remains circular/self-similar in shape) rather than distorting asymmetrically. There is NO spatially-varying random field with independent degrees of freedom within a segment, and NO representation of eccentric, off-axis, or otherwise asymmetric lumen shape error anywhere in the paper. The paper itself states the scope restriction (Section 1): "We limit the developments of this paper to uncertainty in lumen radius, since that is hypothesized to be the primary variable driving pressure loss near diseased regions... We also only captured uncertainty in the lumen area" (Section 4). This is the same one-random-variable-per-section, uniform-dilation/erosion scheme as the MICCAI 2014 and IEEE TMI 2015 sibling papers — richer only in that Section 3.2 additionally tests robustness of the sensitivity RESULT to the CHOICE of segment granularity (55/150/300/600 segments), not in the type of perturbation applied within a segment.

**2. Does it perturb TOPOLOGY in any way — missed/spurious branches, connectivity changes, truncation? Quote any passage on missed branches or bifurcations, or state plainly that topology is never perturbed.**
Topology is never perturbed; it is explicitly held fixed by construction. The correlation length σ for the Gaussian perturbation is deliberately set "so that the segment ends are at a distance 3σ from center with negligible perturbation" (Section 2.3) — i.e., engineered to vanish at bifurcation/segment boundaries. The uniform perturbation form is explicitly rejected for 3D simulation for the same topology-preserving reason: "The latter does not ensure C 0 continuity of the surface at bifurcation locations, and hence cannot be used with 3D simulations." And directly in Section 1: "The validation of the perturbation model is restricted to deformation maps with fixed bifurcation locations." No missed branch, spurious branch, truncation, or any other connectivity/topological perturbation is described, tested, or even mentioned anywhere in the paper (confirmed by full-text search — zero hits for "topolog," "side-branch," "missed branch," or equivalent terms). The Discussion's future-work paragraph names bifurcation deformation as an OPEN problem, not something solved here: "The latter can also help define deformation maps at bifurcation locations for the 3D geometry" (Section 4) — implying the current work's bifurcation locations are NOT deformable/perturbable.

**3. Where do the uncertainty MAGNITUDES come from? Quote the source exactly. Real human inter-observer measurement, inter-modality comparison, expert elicitation, or assumed? If any real data, give N, number of observers, and the metric.**
Assumed/engineering value, with no cited empirical source of any kind — not real inter-observer, not inter-modality, not even a cited expert-elicitation study. Quoted (Section 2.4.1): "The coefficient u 0 in Eq. (5) is defined as a uniform function in the stochastic space, i.e. u 0 ∼ U[0, u max ], where u max is the maximum uncertainty defined usually as a percentage of the radius." No citation, dataset, or measurement is attached to this definition. The only concrete numeric values used in the paper are working parameters chosen for demonstration, not measured quantities: "A perturbation magnitude of 20% is used" (Section 3.3.2, for the ML-vs-3D verification), and Fig. 12's caption compares "20%, 40%, and 60%" perturbations. A full-text search of this paper confirms there is NO mention anywhere of "inter-observer," "inter-reader," "inter-rater," "reproducibility," OCT, IVUS, or any other real segmentation-disagreement dataset — this is a meaningfully weaker empirical grounding for uncertainty magnitude than even the sibling 2016 J. Biomech. paper (which cites a 97-lesion/23-patient OCT-vs-CTA inter-MODALITY comparison, itself not inter-observer). In this CMAME 2015 paper, magnitude sourcing is NOT REPORTED beyond "usually as a percentage of the radius" — i.e., purely assumed.

**4. What are the OUTPUTS? Give every reported numeric result. Is any result a per-case reclassification across FFR 0.80, or is everything continuous (standard deviation, variance, confidence interval, sensitivity index)? Answer this explicitly and quote the strongest candidate passage.**
Every reported output is continuous; there is no per-case/per-lesion reclassification result anywhere in the paper. Full numeric inventory: mesh convergence <2% max FFRCT error at ~4M elements vs ~40M reference (20 patients, Section 3.1.1); four-parameter stochastic convergence tolerance ϵ=0.01 with relative-error statistic κ combining mean, standard deviation, and CI90 differences vs. Monte-Carlo (Section 3.1.2); ML-regressor-vs-3D-simulation correlation ρ=0.909–0.943, MAE=0.026–0.035, RMS=0.040–0.103 across DiscoverFlow(90)/DeFACTO(240) train/test combinations (Table 3, Section 3.3.1); ML-vs-3D sensitivity correlation ρ=0.92, MAE=0.014, RMS=0.018 over 20 patients/30 segments at 20% perturbation (Section 3.3.2, Fig. 8); qualitative segment-sensitivity ranking maps (Figs. 10, 11) and erosion/dilation asymmetry plots (Figs. 12, 13). The only appearance of the 0.80 threshold anywhere in the text is purely motivational, in the Introduction, with no accompanying data: "For instance, a cutoff of 0.8 in FFRCT is used to determine the treatment protocol. Uncertainty quantification (UQ) tools can help quantify confidence of diagnosis using FFRCT that is calculated through simulation" (Section 1). This sentence sets up the CLINICAL STAKES of the 0.80 cutoff but is never followed by any per-case reclassification count, confusion matrix, or rate of lesions crossing 0.80 due to geometric perturbation — the actual output machinery (σFFRCT, correlation coefficients, MAE/RMS) is entirely continuous/aggregate.

**5. BOUNDARY CONDITIONS: how are they set? Are they re-derived, re-tuned, or held fixed when geometry is perturbed? Quote the procedure. Is there ANY tuned-vs-untuned comparison, or any statement that BC tuning compensates for / masks / absorbs geometric error?**
Boundary conditions (outlet resistances) are computed once, from patient-specific clinical/anatomic data, independent of the geometric-perturbation experiments — there is no re-tuning, no re-derivation from a corrupted geometry, and no tuned-vs-untuned comparison anywhere in the paper. Setup (Section 2.2): "the outlets are modeled using a resistance condition which couples blood pressure and velocity at outlet nodes." The resistance-assignment procedure (Appendix B) computes net downstream resistance Reff from outlet areas and calibrates the proportionality constant k against Paorta and coronary flow rate qcor derived from patient height/weight/myocardial mass/blood pressure (BSA, aortic flow Q, coronary flow qcor formulas, Section 2.5.1) — a population-anatomic-scaling method, not a per-case iterative tuning-to-target procedure, and not re-run in response to a geometric perturbation in any of the reported experiments. Nowhere does the paper describe perturbing geometry and then re-solving/re-tuning the resistance BC to compensate; nowhere does it discuss or test whether BC tuning masks or absorbs geometric error. The only compensating physical mechanism discussed is intrinsic to the physics, not a BC re-tuning action (Section 4): "Blood flow rate through the entire model depends on lumped boundary resistance and geometric resistance of the model... segments with large radius which are critical to blood transport are more sensitive to erosion, and diseased and segments far from aorta are more sensitive to dilation" — this describes how FIXED downstream resistance interacts with a perturbed segmental resistance (a series-circuit effect), not a re-optimization of the BC parameter itself.

**6. What is the UQ and ML machinery (stochastic collocation, sparse grids, surrogates, adaptive refinement)? Cohort size, real patients or idealised, invasive FFR ground truth?**
UQ machinery: adaptive stochastic collocation with the Smolyak sparse-grid algorithm, 1D nested Chebyshev collocation nodes, and a custom frozen/active-point adaptive refinement scheme (Section 2.4.1, Algorithm 1) that "split[s] collocation points into a frozen set and an active set," terminating "when all collocation points are frozen." ML surrogate: bootstrap-aggregated (10-tree) decision-tree regressor with information-gain (entropy) splitting on ~30+ geometric/clinical/reduced-order-model features (Table 2, Section 2.5–2.6), trained/tested via Weka. Cohorts: real patients from two multicenter clinical trials — DiscoverFlow (90 patients) and DeFACTO (240 patients) — used reciprocally for ML training/testing (Table 3); 20 patients for mesh convergence; 20 patients/30 segments for ML-vs-3D sensitivity verification; five patients from the DeFACTO test set for sensitivity-field visualization (Fig. 11); one patient-specific dataset for the segment-granularity robustness test (Section 3.2) and for the erosion/dilation asymmetry comparison (Fig. 12). All cohorts are real patients, not idealized/synthetic geometries. Invasive FFR ground truth: DiscoverFlow and DeFACTO are established invasive-FFR-validated trials (refs [11],[12]), but this paper's own reported accuracy metric (Table 3, ρ=0.909–0.943) is ML-surrogate-vs-3D-CFD-simulated FFRCT agreement, NOT ML-vs-invasive-FFR agreement — invasive FFR ground truth is never directly compared against in this paper's own reported numbers.

**7. Does it make any claim about which geometric regions or features matter most — i.e. a RANKING of any kind? Quote it and say exactly what is being ranked (regions? variables? features? error types?).**
Yes — two distinct rankings are made, both of SEGMENTS/REGIONS and of ML FEATURES, never of geometric ERROR TYPES (since only one error type — area dilation/erosion — exists in this paper). (i) Segment/region sensitivity ranking (Abstract, Section 3.4, Fig. 10): "Segments with significant disease in the larger arteries had the highest sensitivities. Terminal segments are more sensitive to dilation and proximal healthy segments are more sensitive to erosion. Sensitivity to geometry is highest when geometric resistance is comparable to net downstream resistance." **9. DECISIVE JUDGEMENT**

- **(a) Segmentation-error magnitudes from REAL MEASURED INTER-OBSERVER disagreement**: **NOT COVERED.** The uncertainty magnitude umax is "defined usually as a percentage of the radius" with no cited measurement of any kind — not inter-observer, not inter-modality, not expert-elicited from a described study. This is strictly weaker empirical grounding than even the sibling 2016 J. Biomech. paper (which at least cites a 97-lesion/23-patient OCT-vs-CTA inter-modality comparison for patient-specific MLD, though that too is not inter-observer). A full-text search confirms zero mentions of inter-observer/inter-reader/inter-rater data anywhere in this paper.

- **(b) DISTINCT error TYPES injected separately (stenosis length, eccentric/off-axis lumen, missed side-branch/topological, taper/undersizing)**: **NOT COVERED.** Exactly one error type exists in this paper — a single per-segment scalar radial area dilation/erosion (Eqs. 4–5), applied either as a localized Gaussian bump (validation arm) or a spatially uniform block (production/ML arm). There is no eccentric/off-axis lumen-shape perturbation (the perturbation is applied uniformly around the circumference, preserving a self-similar cross-section), no missed-branch/topological perturbation (bifurcation locations are explicitly fixed by construction), and no distinct taper/undersizing error type separable from plain dilation/erosion. Section 3.2's four-way test varies only the SPATIAL GRANULARITY of segmentation (55 vs. 600 segments), not the TYPE of error.

- **(c) PER-CASE RECLASSIFICATION across the binary FFR ≤ 0.80 threshold**: **NOT COVERED.** Every reported output is continuous (σFFRCT standard deviation, correlation coefficients, MAE/RMS, and CI90 as one term in a convergence-diagnostic statistic). The 0.80 cutoff appears exactly once, in the Introduction, purely as motivational framing ("a cutoff of 0.8 in FFRCT is used to determine the treatment protocol") with zero accompanying reclassification data, confusion matrix, or count of lesions/cases crossing the threshold anywhere in Sections 3 or 4.

- **(d) RE-TUNING outlet boundary conditions AFTER a geometry change, testing whether re-tuning ABSORBS/MASKS the geometric error**: **NOT COVERED.** Outlet resistances are computed once from patient-specific anatomic/clinical scaling data (Appendix B) and are never re-derived or re-tuned in response to a geometric perturbation in any experiment reported. No tuned-vs-untuned BC comparison exists, and no statement anywhere claims or examines BC tuning masking/absorbing geometric error; the only "compensation" discussed is the passive series-circuit interaction between fixed downstream resistance and a perturbed segmental (geometric) resistance — a physics observation, not a re-tuning procedure.

**Bottom line.** This CMAME 2015 paper is materially the SAME underlying geometric-uncertainty framework as the MICCAI 2014 and IEEE TMI 2015 sibling papers (identical one-random-variable-per-segment, uniform/Gaussian area-perturbation scheme, same fixed-bifurcation restriction, same continuous σFFRCT sensitivity output), republished/extended in a computational-mechanics venue with the ADDITIONAL contributions of (i) a machine-learning (decision-tree) surrogate trained on DiscoverFlow(90)/DeFACTO(240) trial data to accelerate the stochastic-collocation sensitivity computation from days to minutes, and (ii) a robustness check on segment-granularity choice (55–600 segments) for the sensitivity FIELD. It is NOT a materially different geometric-uncertainty model, does NOT add topology or distinct error types, does NOT add reclassification analysis, and does NOT add any BC re-tuning experiment — on all four of T6's target contributions (a)–(d) it is NOT COVERED, and in fact its empirical grounding for uncertainty magnitude (a) is weaker than the already-inadequate 2016 sibling. This paper therefore LEAVES INTACT a study built on the conjunction of (a)–(d) and a three-protocol BC ablation (fixed / re-derived-from-corrupted-geometry / physiologically-constrained target-matched) crossed with distinct error types — nothing in this paper competes with any of the four legs. The single most dangerous sentence in the paper for that study is not a result that scoops T6, but a scope-defining sentence that shows how entrenched and unchallenged the single-scalar/no-topology/no-reclassification convention already is across this author group's whole line of work: "We limit the developments of this paper to uncertainty in lumen radius, since that is hypothesized to be the primary variable driving pressure loss near diseased regions... The validation of the perturbation model is restricted to deformation maps with fixed bifurcation locations." A reviewer familiar with this HeartFlow/Sankaran lineage could use this sentence to ask why, a decade later, T6 still needs to justify departing from lumen-radius-only, fixed-topology geometric uncertainty — but the sentence itself confirms, rather than undermines, that T6's departure (error types, topology, reclassification, BC ablation) has not yet been taken by this paper.
