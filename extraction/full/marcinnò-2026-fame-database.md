## Problem & claim
- Imaging modality and domain: Invasive X-ray coronary angiography (ICA), two near-orthogonal 2D projections per vessel, reconstructed into 3D patient-specific coronary artery geometries; cardiac/coronary haemodynamics.
- Task (segmentation / registration / detection / classification / generation / other): Other — release of a database of 3D coronary meshes and their corresponding unsteady CFD (Navier-Stokes/FEM) simulation results, built on an upstream 2D-to-3D reconstruction and a spline-based hexahedral mesh generator; not itself a segmentation or learning method paper.
- Stated gap the paper attacks: "the field still lacks database[s] based on realistic anatomies. This data scarcity slows the translation of computational hæmodynamics into clinical research." Also: "the scarcity of publicly available numerical hæmodynamics data, despite the growing interest in data-driven modeling and machine learning techniques."
- Central claim in one sentence: The authors release, at large scale (914 reconstructed vessels, 779 successfully meshed and simulated), a public database of hexahedral meshes with fixed vertex/cell count and identical connectivity plus their FEM Navier-Stokes simulation results for real coronary arteries from the FAME 2 trial, together with the first systematic mesh-quality evaluation of a mesh generator at this scale in numerical haemodynamics.

## Method
- Architecture / algorithm, in enough detail to reimplement at a block level:
  1. **Angiogram acquisition/frame selection**: "For a given ICA projection, a single frame is extracted from the recorded ICA video sequence at a fixed time point (end diastole - moment of maximal cardiac muscle relaxation)."
  2. **Camera geometry registration**: "Each ICA imaging system (source and detector) is approximately placed in 3D space according to the parameters of acquisition encoded in the ICA DICOM file (primary and secondary angle, X-ray source and detector position), and the relative position of one of the two geometries is optimised in displacement to align three keypoints selected in both images."
  3. **Manual 2D segmentation**: "The coronary artery of interest is segmented manually from each image using a customised tablet and stylus by board-certified interventional cardiologists, Mahendiran et al. (2024), thus providing arterial thickness information (including identifying areas of stenosis) as well as the arterial centre line. The artery is described as a centreline cubic spline evolving in x and y (the pixels of the detector) as well as t (thickness in pixels)."
  4. **3D centreline reconstruction**: "The 3D reconstruction of the centreline simply relies on the matching of spline-parameters between the two splines respecting epipolar intersections. Matching points uniquely define a 3D point in space."
  5. **Radius/surface generation**: "the arterial radius is reconstructed by averaging the thickness from both views, obtaining a circular cross section" — the vessel surface is then generated by applying this estimated thickness profile to the reconstructed 3D centreline.
  6. **Validity check**: geometries checked for self-intersection; 135/914 discarded (see Q1 below), 779 retained.
  7. **Mesh generation**: structured hexahedral meshes generated with "the algorithm presented in Marcinnò et al. (2025)" (a spline-based hexahedral mesh generator), output in Gmsh format with inlet, outlet, wall, and volume regions pre-tagged; all 779 valid meshes forced to identical vertex count, cell count, and connectivity (95,680 cells each).
  8. **Simulation**: 3D unsteady incompressible Navier–Stokes, FEM (Q1-Q1 elements, VMS-SUPG + backflow stabilization), Neumann inlet pressure from an offline closed-loop 0D whole-cardiovascular model, lumped-parameter (Kim et al. 2010) coronary outlet model, no-slip walls, zero initial condition, solved with lifeX.
- Key technical novelty (what specifically is new vs. the closest prior work): Scale and completeness — "To the best of our knowledge, in the context of numerical hæmodynamics this is the first systematic evaluation of a mesh generator conducted on such a large number of elements; we also remark that except for the SimVascular repository ... we are not aware of any other database that provides both simulations and meshes for real coronary arteries." All meshes forced to identical connectivity/vertex count, useful for ML.
- Loss functions, training regime, augmentation, pre-training: NOT REPORTED / not applicable — no learned model is trained in this paper; the mesh generator and CFD solver are deterministic numerical pipelines.
- Inputs/outputs, dimensionality, resolution: Input — two near-orthogonal 2D ICA projection frames (DICOM) per vessel, manually segmented centreline+thickness. Output per vessel — a structured hexahedral volume mesh (95,680 cells, fixed connectivity), a per-cell mesh-quality .csv, and a full unsteady 3D velocity/pressure field simulation over one cardiac cycle (dt = 0.001 s, T = 1 s simulated, first 0.2 s discarded as ramp-up) stored as solution_*.xdmf/.h5, plus solver log (output.txt), and derived energy/enstrophy quantities (fluid_dynamics.txt).

## Limitations
- Limitations the authors admit (verbatim):
  - "We mention that a main limitation of our simulations is the use of the same values for the lumped parameters of the outlet boundary condition for each patient. While both the geometry and fluid dynamics are correctly modeled thanks to the spline-based mesh generator and the unsteady NS equations, a proper calibration of the boundary conditions should be performed, especially when a stenosis is present. This issue will be investigated in a future work."
  - On the 18 non-converged simulations: "Among the 779 simulations, 18 exhibited convergence issues, i.e. the linear solver reaches the maximum number of iterations (5000). We believe these issues arise primarily due to the simplicity of these 18 geometries, mostly cylindrical with only slight curvature. In particular, such geometries appear to be more susceptible to the tuning of the outlet boundary condition parameters, leading to oscillatory behavior and convergence problems. Nevertheless, we decided to leave these simulations inside the database."
  - On excluded geometries: "The reconstructed geometries may contain inaccuracies arising from errors introduced during upstream processing or from violations of the underlying assumptions. Potential sources of reconstruction error include inaccurate alignment of the estimated 3D displacements, violation of the assumption that the observed motion consists solely of displacement, and inaccuracies in temporal correspondence."
- Limitations I can see that they do NOT admit: (1) No quantitative comparison of the discarded (self-intersecting) geometries against any ground truth or independent imaging modality (e.g., CT) to determine whether the self-intersection reflects true anatomical geometry, segmentation error, or reconstruction-algorithm error specifically — the paper attributes cause narratively (radius-to-curvature ratio, RCA tortuosity) without a quantitative error decomposition. (2) No inter-observer/inter-segmenter agreement is reported for the manual 2D tablet-and-stylus segmentation step that feeds the reconstruction, so the paper cannot separate "bad segmentation" from "bad reconstruction algorithm" as the cause of self-intersections. (3) Bifurcations are explicitly excluded ("The dataset is composed of single branches (LAD, LCx, and RCA) and bifurcations are not taken into account") without discussion of how this restricts the anatomical generality of the reported failure/discard rates. (4) No WSS, OSI, or other spatially-resolved haemodynamic descriptor is computed or validated, despite the stated motivation of enabling downstream haemodynamic/ML analyses that typically rely on such fields. (5) No patient-specific inlet/outlet boundary condition tuning or validation against invasive pressure/flow data is performed, despite FFR (a pressure-ratio measurement) being available for the same patients in the source FAME 2 trial.

## Openings
- Explicit "future work" statements, quoted: "a proper calibration of the boundary conditions should be performed, especially when a stenosis is present. This issue will be investigated in a future work." "By releasing both the meshes and the numerical simulations, we aim to give researchers the flexibility to run their own analyses, change the numerical settings, or test different boundary conditions."
- 3–6 unresolved questions this paper leaves open, each one sentence:
  1. Whether the 135 self-intersecting geometries are self-intersecting because of true (segmenter-independent) reconstruction/registration error, or because of manual 2D segmentation error at the tablet-and-stylus step, is never disentangled.
  2. Whether patient-specific (rather than vessel-type-averaged LAD/LCx vs RCA) lumped-parameter outlet boundary conditions, calibrated e.g. against the trial's own invasive FFR measurements, would change the simulated haemodynamics or the 18 non-converged cases.
  3. Whether WSS, OSI, or other spatially-resolved wall-haemodynamic fields — not computed here — could be derived from the released velocity/pressure fields and how they would look given the fixed-connectivity mesh.
  4. Whether the self-intersection/discard rate would fall if bifurcations were included, or whether including bifurcations would raise it further given already-higher RCA failure rates tied to tortuosity.
  5. Whether the mesh-generation algorithm (Marcinnò et al. 2025) could itself repair or regularise near-self-intersecting surfaces rather than requiring outright discard, since no repair/smoothing step is described in this paper.

## T6 feasibility questions

**1. Quote the exact passage about discarded/self-intersecting/failed geometries. How many were discarded, out of how many total? What was the stated failure mode?**

Abstract: "Out of the 914 clinical images, 779 vessels are successfully reconstructed and meshed. The remaining 135 vessels have been discarded since they exhibited self-intersecting geometry during the reconstruction process."

Section 3 (The FAME2 Dataset): "In total, 914 vessel geometries were reconstructed from these patients. Of these, 135 contained self-intersections and were therefore excluded from the meshing procedure, leaving 779 vessels suitable for mesh generation. The self-intersecting geometries are nevertheless retained in the database."

Numbers: **135 discarded out of 914 total reconstructed vessel geometries** (14.77%), leaving 779 meshed/simulated. Broken down by vessel type (Table 1): LAD 32/385 (8.3%), LCx 35/274 (12.7%), RCA 68/255 (26.6%).

Stated failure mode: "self-intersecting geometry during the reconstruction process" — i.e., the failure is a **surface self-intersection**, not a meshing-algorithm crash. Mechanistically: "These errors may lead to self-intersections when the vessel surface is generated by applying the estimated thickness profile to the reconstructed 3D centreline. Such self-intersections occur primarily in regions where the vessel radius is large relative to the local radius of curvature."

**2. What produced these geometries (which segmentation method/tool, which imaging source)? Were failures traced to segmentation quality, to anatomy, or to the meshing step itself?**

Imaging source: invasive X-ray coronary angiography (ICA), two near-orthogonal projections per vessel from the FAME 2 trial. Segmentation method: manual — "The coronary artery of interest is segmented manually from each image using a customised tablet and stylus by board-certified interventional cardiologists, Mahendiran et al. (2024)" — producing a centreline-plus-thickness spline per 2D view, subsequently matched via epipolar geometry into a 3D centreline, then given a circular cross-section from averaged thickness.

Failures were traced to the **upstream 2D→3D reconstruction step**, not explicitly to segmentation quality per se and not to the mesher: "Potential sources of reconstruction error include inaccurate alignment of the estimated 3D displacements, violation of the assumption that the observed motion consists solely of displacement, and inaccuracies in temporal correspondence." Anatomy is explicitly implicated as a secondary driver: "Self-intersections were most frequent in the RCA, occurring in 26.6% of RCA reconstructions, compared with 12.7% for the LCx and 8.3% for the LAD. This higher incidence may be related to the RCA's characteristically tortuous, C-shaped anatomy, which makes angiographic reconstruction more susceptible to centreline overlap and foreshortening in the projected images." The paper never attributes any of the 135 discards to the **mesh generator** itself — the mesher was never run on those 135 (they were excluded before meshing), and none of the 779 valid geometries are reported to have failed meshing.

**10. FEASIBILITY JUDGEMENT: from this paper's own numbers, what BASE RATE of automated meshing failure should a study expect on ordinary (uncorrupted) patient coronary geometries? State the rate and the arithmetic. Then reason explicitly about what this implies for DELIBERATELY CORRUPTED geometries, and flag clearly that the corrupted-case rate is an extrapolation not measured by this paper.**

**Base rate, as measured by this paper:** 135 discarded out of 914 reconstructed vessel geometries = 135/914 = **14.8%** overall pooled discard rate for self-intersecting geometry, on ordinary (not deliberately corrupted) real patient coronary anatomy. By vessel type, the rate is highly anatomy-dependent: LAD 32/385 = 8.3%, LCx 35/274 = 12.8%, RCA 68/255 = 26.7% — i.e., roughly a 3x spread between the least- and most-tortuous vessel types, driven by the RCA's "characteristically tortuous, C-shaped anatomy."

**Important qualification on what this rate actually measures**: this is NOT a rate of the hex-mesher itself choking on a valid input surface. The mesher was never invoked on the 135 discarded cases — self-intersection was detected and the case discarded *before* mesh generation was attempted ("135 contained self-intersections and were therefore excluded from the meshing procedure"). Once a geometry passed the self-intersection check, meshing itself appears to have been essentially 100% successful and fast (779/779 valid geometries meshed, all with identical topology/cell count, in an aggregate 41 minutes for 915 attempted meshes). So the correct reading of this paper's evidence is: **the dominant failure mode in an automated coronary geometry-to-mesh pipeline is upstream, at surface generation/reconstruction (self-intersection), not at the hex-meshing step itself** — and that upstream failure rate on ordinary patient anatomy is ~15% pooled, ranging 8–27% by vessel.

**Implication for T6's deliberately corrupted geometries (EXTRAPOLATION — not measured by this paper):** The paper's own stated mechanism for self-intersection is that it "occur[s] primarily in regions where the vessel radius is large relative to the local radius of curvature" — i.e., self-intersection is driven by local radius/curvature mismatch. Several of T6's planned corruption types (eccentric lumen, stenosis-length change, taper change) directly manipulate local radius and its rate of change along the vessel, which is exactly the geometric quantity this paper identifies as the proximate cause of self-intersection; a missed side branch changes local topology/curvature at the branch site. It is therefore reasonable to expect that deliberately injecting these perturbations onto already-reconstructed geometries would push the self-intersection/failure rate for automated surface generation and meshing *above* this paper's ~15% (pooled) to ~27% (RCA-like tortuous-vessel worst case) natural-anatomy baseline — plausibly substantially above it, especially for RCA-like or already-tortuous source geometries, since the corruption is targeting the very geometric parameter (radius vs. local curvature) this paper flags as causal. However, **this is an extrapolation**: this paper never tests deliberately corrupted or perturbed geometries, never reports a rate for post-hoc-edited (as opposed to natively reconstructed) surfaces, and its 135/914 figure describes failures arising from noisy 2D-to-3D reconstruction of unaltered patient anatomy, not from deliberate geometric editing of an already-valid 3D surface. T6 should treat 15–27% as a plausible *floor* for expected automated-meshing-pipeline failure/rework rate on corrupted cases, not as a measured value, and should budget contingency (manual surface repair, case exclusion/replacement, or extra schedule) accordingly at their planned 15–20 case scale, where even a 15% base failure rate implies 2–3 cases requiring rework and a 25–30% corrupted-case rate could imply 4–6 of 15–20 cases requiring intervention — arithmetic illustration only, not a value taken from this paper.

## Summary
- 135/914 (14.8%) of reconstructed coronary vessel geometries from the FAME 2 database were discarded for self-intersection before meshing was even attempted; once past that check, hex-meshing itself succeeded on essentially all (779/779) remaining cases in an aggregate 41 minutes.
- The failure mode is self-intersecting surface geometry from 2D ICA-to-3D reconstruction (attributed to reconstruction/registration error and to anatomy — RCA tortuosity gave 26.6% failure vs 8.3% for LAD), not a meshing-algorithm crash on an otherwise-valid surface.
- No surface-repair or smoothing step is reported anywhere; bad geometries are simply excluded, not fixed.
- Boundary conditions are not patient-specific (only two parameter sets, by LAD/LCx vs RCA), and no WSS/OSI or other spatially-resolved wall field is computed — only velocity, pressure, kinetic energy and enstrophy.
- The paper's own base rate (~15% pooled, up to 27% for tortuous vessels) is direct evidence only for ordinary anatomy; applying it to T6's deliberately corrupted geometries is an explicit extrapolation, justified by the paper's own stated radius/curvature mechanism but not measured by it.
