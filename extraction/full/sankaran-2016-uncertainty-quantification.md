## Problem & claim
- Imaging modality and domain: Coronary CT angiography (cCTA)-derived patient-specific 3D coronary artery models; cross-referenced against invasive OCT (for MLD/MLA ground truth), IVUS (for boundary-resistance ground truth cohort selection), and invasive FFR (for boundary-resistance calibration and for comparison to measurement reproducibility).
- Task: Other — forward uncertainty propagation / uncertainty quantification (UQ) through a 3D CFD (Navier–Stokes) blood-flow and FFRCT simulation pipeline, using an adaptive stochastic collocation method, not a segmentation or classification task itself.
- Stated gap the paper attacks (quoted): "The main goal of this work is to understand the impact of uncertainties in lumen geometry (minimum lumen diameter and lesion length), boundary conditions and blood viscosity on the blood flow and pressures in the coronary artery. We investigate the relative importance of each of these model parameters and calculate the impact of these on FFRCT, in comparison with measurement variability. Finally, we perform a combined uncertainty quantification analysis where all variables are perturbed simultaneously to determine whether the uncertainty in the parameters is additive."
- Central claim in one sentence: Using a data-driven adaptive stochastic-collocation UQ framework on an idealized stenosis model and one patient-specific model, uncertainty in minimum lumen diameter (MLD) has the largest impact on FFRCT of the four modeled variables (MLD, lesion length, boundary resistance, viscosity), the resulting FFRCT uncertainty near the 0.8 cutoff is generally within or only slightly above the reproducibility of invasively measured FFR, and combined multi-variable uncertainty is not additive — it is only marginally larger than the single largest-contributing variable (MLD) alone.
- Stated novelty vs. prior work (quoted): "The differences between this work and prior work (Sankaran et al., 2015, 2015) are (i) in the previous work, we used a machine learning surrogate for FFRCT whereas we use the Navier–Stokes equations directly in this paper, (ii) uncertainties in minimum lumen diameter, lesion length, boundary resistance and viscosity are all included in this paper, whereas only geometric uncertainty was discussed earlier, and (iii) the input uncertainty model for all the parameters is computed using a data-driven approach."

## Method
- Governing equations: Incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, rigid vessel walls, Newtonian fluid (viscosity 0.04 g/cm·s baseline, density 1.06 g/cm³), solved via finite elements (cites Taylor et al. 1998; Kim et al. 2010; Jansen et al. 2000).
- Geometries studied (two only):
  1. **Idealized stenosis model**: "a constant diameter cylindrical vessel with a focal stenosis," parameterized by a cosine-taper diameter function d(z) with healthy diameter dh=3.5 mm, stenosis start/end zl=17.5 mm / zu=25 mm, throat location zc, half-length Δ, and modeled % stenosis α (Eq. 1). Constant-pressure inlet BC.
  2. **Patient-specific stenosis model**: one patient, reconstructed from cCTA — "The ascending aorta is first extracted and the coronary ostia identified. Next, the centerlines of the vessels of interest are extracted and the coronary lumen boundary is segmented for each vessel. Finally, the outlet boundaries are defined by trimming the model at pre-defined locations." Has "a focal lesion in each of the main coronary vessels (left circumflex - LCx, left anterior descending - LAD and right coronary artery - RCA)." Parabolic velocity inlet profile at aortic inlet based on target cardiac output.
- Boundary conditions (both geometries): outlets modeled with a resistance condition coupling pressure and flow rate (cites Kim et al. 2010; Vignon-Clementel et al. 2006; Sankaran et al. 2012; Taylor et al. 2013 for the baseline/assigned resistance-assignment method).
- FFRCT computed as FFRCT(x) = Pc(x)/Paorta from steady flow simulations, where Paorta is mean aortic pressure and Pc(x) is mean coronary pressure (Taylor et al., 2013 method).
- UQ machinery: Stochastic Navier–Stokes equations (velocity/pressure as functions of space, time, AND a stochastic variable ξ: u(x,t,ξ), p(x,t,ξ)), solved via **adaptive stochastic collocation** using the **adaptive Smolyak sparse-grid quadrature algorithm** (cites Sankaran and Marsden 2011; Xiu and Hesthaven 2005; Babuska et al. 2007; Klimke 2006; Sankaran and Marsden 2010; Sankaran 2009). 3D Navier–Stokes solved directly at each quadrature point (not a surrogate — explicitly contrasted with their own prior machine-learning-surrogate paper). Simulation counts: "between 5 and 13 simulations for single parameter uncertainty quantification and between 17 and 25 simulations for the combined uncertainty quantification."
- Procedure: "We first perturb each of the four variables separately, fixing the others at their mean value. Then, we perform a combined uncertainty quantification analysis, whereby all the four variables are perturbed simultaneously." A four-dimensional stochastic space is defined for the combined case. Sensitivities (standard deviations) in FFRCT due to the four variables are "compared and ranked." Confidence intervals extracted. Individual sensitivities compared with sensitivities of the combined UQ model.

## Data
- Cohorts used for calibrating the four uncertainty MODELS (not for validating the UQ framework's diagnostic output against a clinical outcome):
  1. **MLD uncertainty (patient-specific model) — Choi et al. 2015 dataset**: "image processing methods were applied to segment lumen boundaries of 97 lesions in 23 patients (Choi et al., 2015). OCT data was co-registered with CT data, and the errors in MLD and MLA were quantified. The mean and standard deviation in error between segmented and ground-truth data was computed and a Gaussian distribution was used to model uncertainty in MLD." (OCT = optical coherence tomography, invasive, used as reference standard.)
  2. **MLD uncertainty (idealized model)**: NOT derived from the OCT data — instead an assumed sub-voxel-accuracy bound: "we use an error of 0.3 mm in the radius, resulting in 0.6 mm error in minimum lumen diameter," modeled as a uniform random variable δ ∈ ±0.3 mm, applied as a spatially UNIFORM dilation/erosion of the whole idealized stenosis ("the entire stenosis model uniformly dilates or erodes spatially, implying that the lumen diameter in the entire vessel reaches their minima and maxima simultaneously").
  3. **Lesion-length uncertainty**: "based on an inter-user variability study, to be ±1 mm, which is modeled as a uniform random variable." No N, no annotator count, no agreement metric given — the "inter-user variability study" is not otherwise cited or described in this paper.
  4. **Boundary-resistance uncertainty**: a cohort of "28 patients where the reconstructed geometry matched invasive imaging data using intra-vascular ultrasound [IVUS]." Procedure: calculate the resistance under hyperemia that makes FFRCT match measured (invasive) FFR for each of these 28 patients; the difference between the assigned resistance (from the standard Taylor et al. 2013 method) and this back-calculated matching resistance is "postulated" to represent boundary-resistance uncertainty (assuming negligible geometric error in this subset). A log-normal distribution (chosen over Gaussian by lower error entropy, fit via Nelder–Mead) was fit to these differences: "lower and upper 95% confidence bounds being 65% and 130% of the mean resistance" (Fig. 1).
  5. **Viscosity uncertainty**: hematocrit modeled as Gaussian, "A mean value of 45 and a standard deviation of 8 was observed" from "values measured in a recent clinical trial (Min et al., 2012)"; viscosity derived from hematocrit via μ = μp/(1-hct/100)^2.5 (μp = plasma viscosity = 0.0011 Pa·s).
- Patient-specific UQ demonstration cohort: **one (1) patient**, three vessels (LAD, LCx, RCA), each with a focal lesion. No invasive FFR ground truth reported for this specific demonstration patient — comparison is instead made against a general reproducibility figure for measured FFR from a separate paper (Gaur et al., 2014: "a variation of 0.03 in measurement FFR").
- Idealized-model "cohort": not real patients — synthetic stenoses swept parametrically from 30% to 80% in 10% increments (Fig. 3).
- Train/val/test split: N/A — not a machine-learning training study.

## Evaluation
- Idealized model, extrema-quadrature-point differences (ξ=0 vs ξ=1) at 60% stenosis (Fig. 4): largest pressure-drop differences due to MLD, lesion length, boundary resistance, hematocrit respectively = **29 mmHg, 6 mmHg, 9 mmHg, 4 mmHg**; corresponding flow-rate differences = **1.3 cc/s, 0.2 cc/s, 0.9 cc/s, 0.2 cc/s**. "The relative importance of the four variables, quantified by their impact either on the pressure drop, FFRCT or flow-rate, averaged across all % stenoses, were (in decreasing order of importance) MLD, boundary resistance, hematocrit and lesion length."
- Idealized model (Fig. 2): "MLD generally exhibiting the highest sensitivity on stenoses greater than 50%. The result of four-dimensional uncertainty quantification analysis is shown in black, and is barely visible since it is only marginally higher than the sensitivity due to MLD."
- Patient-specific model (Fig. 5, standard deviation of FFRCT by variable, per vessel): "MLD has the highest impact in the LAD and LCx followed by boundary resistance, blood viscosity and lesion length. This trend was not observed in the RCA, though the magnitude of uncertainty in FFRCT was generally small (less than 0.03)." "The combined uncertainty including all four variables for LAD, LCx and RCA were **0.037, 0.023 and 0.024** respectively, which is significantly lower than their additive sums." Comparator dotted lines = measurement reproducibility of ±0.03 (Gaur et al., 2014).
- Comparison to measurement reproducibility (quoted, Abstract): "We show that near the diagnostic cutoff (FFRCT = 0.8), the uncertainty due to the latter three variables [boundary resistance, viscosity, lesion length] are lower than measurement uncertainty, while the uncertainty due to MLD is only slightly higher than measurement uncertainty." Also in Results: "only uncertainty in MLD is slightly higher than measurement uncertainty."
- No Sobol indices, no variance-decomposition fractions, and no formal sensitivity coefficients are reported anywhere — ranking is by comparing standard deviations / pressure-drop / flow-rate magnitudes per variable, not a variance-based global sensitivity analysis.
- No confusion matrix, no reclassification count, no per-lesion FFR≤0.80 threshold-crossing analysis is reported. FFRCT distributions and confidence intervals are shown (Fig. 6) but not tabulated with an accuracy/reclassification metric.
- Statistical testing: none reported (no p-values, no formal hypothesis tests); results reported as computed standard deviations, confidence intervals, and probability density functions (PDFs) from the stochastic-collocation sampling.
- Compute reported: NOT REPORTED (no GPU/hardware, no wall-clock time); only simulation-COUNT is reported (5–13 for single-parameter UQ, 17–25 for combined UQ).

## Limitations
- Limitations the authors admit (quoted, Discussion): "we did not consider systolic and diastolic differences in modeling uncertainty in radius." "The uncertainty model depends on the particular algorithm used to compute lumen segmentation from cCTA images." "Uncertainty in boundary conditions was a bit challenging, since they are not measurable directly. In this work, we assumed that ground truth boundary conditions are those that match measured pressure data within the coronary arteries, on cases with accurate geometry... However, our work assumed that the patients do not have microvascular dysfunction, in which case the magnitude of uncertainty in resistance could be higher." "With more data, the use of a localized image quality metric to define input uncertainties would result in a more accurate assessment of geometric uncertainty." On the idealized model's uniform-dilation assumption: "it is unrealistic to assume that uncertainty in minimum lumen diameter is correlated across the entire geometry, which implies a much larger error in geometry obtained from CT scans than those reported in literature" — i.e., the authors explicitly flag their own idealized-model geometric perturbation (uniform whole-vessel dilation/erosion) as an overestimate/unrealistic correlation assumption, though they argue this only strengthens their conclusion that even this "rigorous" (worst-case) modeling gives modest uncertainty near the 0.8 cutoff. Small demonstration N: "we picked a patient specific example where the FFRCT was near the 0.8 cutoff" — i.e., a single hand-picked patient, not a validation cohort.
- Limitations I can see that they do NOT admit: (1) Only ONE patient-specific case is used to demonstrate/validate the whole framework — no cohort-level reclassification, accuracy, or generalizability analysis is possible from this. (2) Geometric uncertainty is treated as a SINGLE scalar per-lesion quantity (MLD, a single diameter number) plus a separate single scalar for lesion length — there is no spatially-varying lumen-boundary perturbation field, no representation of eccentricity/off-axis lumen shape error, and no representation of missed side-branches or topological error; the four "variables" are MLD, lesion length, boundary resistance, and viscosity, i.e., a global focal-narrowing severity plus a global stenosis-extent value, not per-section or per-error-type geometric error. (3) The idealized-model geometric perturbation is explicitly a spatially-UNIFORM whole-vessel dilation/erosion (correlated across the entire geometry), not a localized/independent per-section perturbation. (4) Lesion length uncertainty is cited to an internal "inter-user variability study" with no N, annotator count, agreement metric, or citation given — it cannot be checked or reproduced from this paper. (5) No branch-loss, eccentricity, or taper/undersizing error types are modeled or even discussed as distinct categories anywhere in the text. (6) Boundary resistance is defined once from a validated 28-patient population-level cohort and then held FIXED (at its baseline/assigned value) while perturbing other variables — there is no experiment in which geometry is deliberately perturbed and then boundary conditions are RE-TUNED/re-optimized to compensate; BC "uncertainty" here means uncertainty in the resistance PARAMETER VALUE itself, not a re-tuning response to a geometry error.

## Openings
- Explicit "future work"/implication statements (quoted): "The framework outlined herein may be useful in calculating the uncertainty in geometry, boundary conditions as well as other parameters such as viscosity in patient-specific models. This would help in reporting standard deviation and confidence intervals, and ultimately interval estimates instead of point estimates. Further, this data may help physicians understand the impact of modeling uncertainty when diagnosing functional significance of coronary artery disease in patients with equivocal diagnostic tests." Also: "with more data, the use of a localized image quality metric to define input uncertainties would result in a more accurate assessment of geometric uncertainty" (implying their current geometric-uncertainty model is NOT localized).
- 3–6 unresolved questions this paper leaves open:
  1. Whether a spatially-varying / localized (per-section) geometric uncertainty field, rather than a single global MLD scalar and a uniformly-dilating idealized model, would change the ranking or magnitude of geometric sensitivity.
  2. Whether distinct geometric ERROR TYPES (eccentricity/off-axis lumen shape, missed side-branch/topological error, taper/undersizing distinct from focal MLD/length) would rank differently from the single MLD+length parameterization used here.
  3. Whether findings generalize beyond the single patient-specific case (3 vessels, 1 patient) demonstrated, given no cohort-level validation is performed.
  4. Whether re-tuning/re-optimizing boundary resistance after a geometric perturbation (rather than treating BC uncertainty as an independent fixed-distribution parameter) would mask or absorb geometric error — this specific tuned-vs-untuned comparison is never attempted.
  5. Whether per-case/per-lesion FFR reclassification across the 0.80 threshold would show a different (more clinically legible) picture than the aggregate standard-deviation/PDF comparisons reported.
  6. Whether the "inter-user variability" lesion-length estimate (±1 mm) and other assumed/expert values (e.g., idealized-model ±0.3 mm radius bound) would hold up against a systematically measured inter-observer/inter-reader segmentation study across multiple readers and cases, as opposed to the single OCT-comparison dataset used for MLD alone.

## T6 decisive questions

**1. Exactly which uncertain inputs were propagated?**
Four variables, stated explicitly: "namely minimum lumen diameter (MLD), lesion length, blood viscosity and boundary resistance" (Section 2, Methods intro). Each parameterised as:
- MLD: idealized model — uniform random variable δ ∈ ±0.3 mm applied to the radius (Eq. 3); patient-specific model — Gaussian distribution fit to OCT-vs-CTA MLD/MLA error (Choi et al., 2015 data).
- Lesion length: uniform random variable, ±1 mm, "based on an inter-user variability study."
- Boundary resistance: log-normal distribution fit (via Nelder–Mead minimizing error entropy) to the difference between assigned resistance and FFRCT-matching resistance in a 28-patient IVUS-validated-geometry cohort; 95% CI bounds = 65%–130% of mean resistance.
- Blood viscosity: derived from hematocrit, modeled as Gaussian (mean 45, SD 8, from Min et al. 2012 clinical trial data), transformed to viscosity via μ = μp/(1-hct/100)^2.5.
No other uncertain inputs (e.g., wall compliance, cardiac output, density) were propagated; walls are explicitly "assumed to be rigid" (fixed, not a UQ variable).

**2. Geometry specifically — how is geometric/lumen uncertainty represented?**
It is a **single global scalar per lesion (or, in the idealized model, a single spatially-UNIFORM perturbation over the whole vessel)** — NOT a spatially-varying field along the vessel and NOT a per-section/localised perturbation, and it does NOT distinguish error types. Quoted: for the idealized model, "We assume that the entire stenosis model uniformly dilates or erodes spatially, implying that the lumen diameter in the entire vessel reaches their minima and maxima simultaneously... In other words, the uncertainty is modeled directly on the vessel size." For the patient-specific model, uncertainty is modeled purely as a distribution over the scalar MLD/MLA error at the single narrowest point: "The mean and standard deviation in error between segmented and ground-truth data was computed and a Gaussian distribution was used to model uncertainty in MLD." Lesion length is a second, separate scalar (±1 mm uniform), representing stenosis EXTENT, not lumen shape. There is no eccentricity/off-axis representation (the model is an axisymmetric diameter function d(z), Eq. 1 — geometry is inherently non-eccentric by construction), no missed side-branch/topological perturbation, and no distinct taper/undersizing parameter — "taper" in this paper simply IS the fixed cosine-shaped diameter profile of the deterministic model (Eq. 1), not an uncertain/perturbed quantity. The authors themselves flag this as unrealistic: "it is unrealistic to assume that uncertainty in minimum lumen diameter is correlated across the entire geometry."

**3. Where do the geometric uncertainty MAGNITUDES come from?**
Mixed — one real measured-disagreement source (patient-specific MLD only) and one assumed/expert-estimate source (idealized-model MLD) and one unspecified-study source (lesion length):
- Patient-specific MLD: REAL measured inter-modality (not inter-observer/inter-reader) disagreement — "image processing methods were applied to segment lumen boundaries of 97 lesions in 23 patients (Choi et al., 2015). OCT data was co-registered with CT data, and the errors in MLD and MLA were quantified." This is CTA-segmentation-vs-OCT-ground-truth error (a single automated/semi-automated segmentation method compared to an invasive imaging reference), not inter-observer/inter-reader agreement between multiple human readers of the same CTA. N=97 lesions, 23 patients; annotators/readers NOT REPORTED (Choi et al. 2015 is a separate paper, not detailed here); agreement metric = mean and SD of MLD/MLA error (a bias/error histogram), not an agreement statistic like ICC, Dice, or Bland-Altman limits reported in THIS paper's text.
- Idealized-model MLD: an ASSUMED value, explicitly not measured — "Since state-of-the-art algorithms can represent the model with sub-voxel accuracy, we use an error of 0.3 mm in the radius" — a reasoned engineering assumption, not a cited empirical measurement.
- Lesion length: cited to "an inter-user variability study" with NO further detail — NOT REPORTED: no N, no annotators, no agreement metric, no citation given anywhere in the text for this specific study.
- Boundary resistance: NOT a geometric quantity — derived from a 28-patient IVUS-matched cohort's FFRCT-vs-measured-FFR back-calculation (see Q1).

**4. Does it rank the relative contribution of geometry vs boundary conditions vs viscosity? Give every numeric result.**
Yes, explicitly ranked, but by comparing standard deviations / pressure-drop / flow-rate magnitudes across the four separately-perturbed variables — **no Sobol indices, no variance-fraction decomposition, and no formal sensitivity coefficients are reported anywhere in the paper.** All numeric results:
- Idealized model, extrema differences at 60% stenosis (Fig. 4): pressure-drop differences due to MLD/length/resistance/hematocrit = 29, 6, 9, 4 mmHg; flow-rate differences = 1.3, 0.2, 0.9, 0.2 cc/s. "The relative importance of the four variables... averaged across all % stenoses, were (in decreasing order of importance) MLD, boundary resistance, hematocrit and lesion length."
- Patient-specific model (Fig. 5/Discussion): "MLD has the highest impact in the LAD and LCx followed by boundary resistance, blood viscosity and lesion length. This trend was not observed in the RCA." Combined (all-4-variable) FFRCT standard deviation for LAD, LCx, RCA = **0.037, 0.023, 0.024** respectively ("significantly lower than their additive sums").
- Abstract/Discussion summary ranking (repeated twice, consistent): MLD > boundary resistance > viscosity > lesion length, in both geometries (with the RCA as a stated exception where magnitudes were all small, <0.03, because deterministic FFRCT there exceeded 0.8).
- No Sobol index, no % variance-explained, and no per-parameter sensitivity coefficient (∂FFRCT/∂parameter) table is given anywhere — the "ranking" is a qualitative ordering of standard-deviation/CI magnitudes and extrema pressure/flow differences, not a formal variance-based global sensitivity analysis.

**5. Does it report FFR, and does it report per-case reclassification across 0.80? Or only continuous FFR uncertainty/CIs?**
**Only continuous FFRCT uncertainty/confidence intervals — no per-case/per-lesion reclassification count across the 0.80 threshold is reported anywhere.** Exact numbers: combined 4-variable FFRCT SD for LAD/LCx/RCA = 0.037/0.023/0.024; comparator measurement-reproducibility benchmark = ±0.03 (Gaur et al., 2014, shown as dotted lines in Fig. 5). Quoted: "only uncertainty in MLD is slightly higher than measurement uncertainty" and "the range of FFRCT near the diagnostic cutoff of 0.8 is within the bounds of reproducibility of measured FFR" (both statements about the SPREAD/CI of FFRCT relative to a benchmark reproducibility figure, not about which side of 0.80 any given lesion's FFRCT PDF crosses, and not a count of lesions/cases that flip classification). Fig. 7 shows one qualitative illustration where the extrema (not the confidence interval) of the LAD's stochastic space span FFRCT ≈0.6 to >0.8 ("The biggest difference is in the LAD where the FFRCT changes from around 0.6 (diseased, top) to more than 0.8 (healthy, bottom). However, since these are extrema in the stochastic space, the actual standard deviation and confidence intervals in FFRCT are significantly lower.") — this is presented as an illustrative extremum, explicitly caveated as not representative of the actual (much smaller) uncertainty, and is not tabulated as a reclassification statistic across any cohort. There is no table of "N lesions changed FFR category" anywhere in the paper, and the demonstration cohort is a single patient (3 vessels), which is too small for a reclassification-rate analysis in any case.

**6. Are boundary conditions RE-TUNED after a geometry perturbation, or held fixed? Any tuned-vs-untuned comparison or masking statement?**
Boundary resistance is treated as an **independent, fixed-distribution stochastic variable perturbed on its own axis of the 4D stochastic space** — it is NOT re-tuned/re-optimized in response to a geometry perturbation anywhere in this paper. The BC "uncertainty model" itself is built once, from a separate calibration cohort (the 28 IVUS-matched patients), by finding "the resistance under hyperemia conditions that would result in a match of FFRCT to the measured FFR" and treating the difference from the assigned (Taylor et al. 2013) resistance as the uncertainty distribution (Fig. 1, log-normal, 65%–130% of mean at 95% CI) — this is a one-time historical/population calibration of BC UNCERTAINTY, not a per-case re-tuning procedure applied after perturbing geometry in the UQ experiments themselves. When MLD (or any other variable) is perturbed in the reported UQ runs, boundary resistance is held at its own independently-sampled point in the stochastic space (or at its mean, for single-parameter runs: "We first perturb each of the four variables separately, fixing the others at their mean value"), not re-solved to compensate for the geometry change. There is **no tuned-vs-untuned comparison and no statement anywhere in the text that BC tuning compensates for or masks geometric error.** If anything, the paper implies the opposite mechanism for why MLD's impact is muted relative to naive expectation — a physical self-regulation of flow, not a BC re-tuning: "there is some self-regulation when considering uncertainty in MLD. A smaller MLD results in higher resistance to flow but also reduces the flow through that segment of the vessel. Hence the net pressure drop is lower than what one might expect assuming flow rate is the same." This is a physiological/fluid-mechanical effect internal to the resistance-outlet-coupled Navier–Stokes solve, not a re-calibration of the resistance BC parameter itself.

**7. UQ machinery, cohort size, real vs idealized, invasive FFR ground truth?**
Machinery: adaptive stochastic collocation using the adaptive Smolyak sparse-grid quadrature algorithm (cites Sankaran and Marsden 2011; Xiu and Hesthaven 2005; Babuska et al. 2007; Klimke 2006), NOT a surrogate model in this paper (explicitly contrasted with their own prior machine-learning-surrogate work, Sankaran et al. 2015). The full 3D Navier–Stokes equations are solved directly at each of the sampled quadrature points. Simulation counts: "between 5 and 13 simulations for single parameter uncertainty quantification and between 17 and 25 simulations for the combined uncertainty quantification." Cohort: two geometries only — (i) one idealized parametric stenosis model swept across 30–80% stenosis severities (not real patients), and (ii) ONE real patient-specific model with three vessels (LAD, LCx, RCA), each with a focal lesion. Separately, three private clinical DATASETS are used only to calibrate the four input-uncertainty distributions (97 lesions/23 patients OCT-CTA for MLD; 28 patients IVUS-matched for boundary resistance; an unnamed hematocrit clinical-trial cohort from Min et al. 2012 for viscosity), not as the UQ demonstration cohort itself. Invasive FFR ground truth: used only in the 28-patient boundary-resistance calibration cohort (to back-calculate the "true" resistance) and as a general reproducibility benchmark (±0.03, from a separate Gaur et al. 2014 study) — NOT reported for the single patient-specific UQ demonstration case itself.

**8. Stated limitations and future work, verbatim.**
See Limitations and Openings sections above for full quotes; key ones: "we did not consider systolic and diastolic differences in modeling uncertainty in radius"; "The uncertainty model depends on the particular algorithm used to compute lumen segmentation from cCTA images"; "our work assumed that the patients do not have microvascular dysfunction, in which case the magnitude of uncertainty in resistance could be higher"; "with more data, the use of a localized image quality metric to define input uncertainties would result in a more accurate assessment of geometric uncertainty"; "it is unrealistic to assume that uncertainty in minimum lumen diameter is correlated across the entire geometry, which implies a much larger error in geometry obtained from CT scans than those reported in literature."

**9. DECISIVE JUDGEMENT on T6's four contributions.**

- **(a) Segmentation-error magnitudes from REAL measured inter-observer disagreement**: **PARTIALLY COVERED.** The patient-specific MLD uncertainty model does use real measured data (Choi et al. 2015, 97 lesions/23 patients), but it is CTA-segmentation-vs-OCT ground-truth error (a single algorithm/reader pipeline vs an invasive reference standard), not INTER-OBSERVER disagreement between multiple human readers/annotators of the same CTA — no inter-reader/inter-observer study is used or reported for MLD in this paper. The lesion-length magnitude (±1 mm) is attributed to "an inter-user variability study" but with zero methodological detail (no N, annotators, or metric), so it cannot be verified as a genuine inter-observer measurement from the text alone. The idealized-model MLD magnitude (±0.3 mm) is explicitly an assumed engineering value, not measured. No eccentricity, branch-loss, or taper/undersizing magnitude is sourced at all (these error types do not exist in this paper's model). Net: one of four/five candidate T6 error magnitudes has a real (but inter-MODALITY, not inter-OBSERVER) empirical source; the rest are assumed, vaguely cited, or absent.

- **(b) DISTINCT error types injected separately (stenosis-length, eccentric/off-axis lumen, missed side-branch/topological, taper/undersizing)**: **NOT COVERED.** This paper injects exactly two geometric-flavoured scalars — a global MLD (severity) perturbation and a separate lesion-length (extent) perturbation — plus two non-geometric variables (boundary resistance, viscosity). There is no eccentricity/off-axis lumen model (geometry is axisymmetric by construction, Eq. 1), no missed-side-branch/topological perturbation, and no distinct taper/undersizing error type (the taper shape is a fixed deterministic profile, not a perturbed quantity). MLD and lesion length are the only two "geometric" axes, and they are global/uniform, not spatially localized or shape-varying. T6's contribution (b) — the very idea of separating distinct geometric ERROR TYPES beyond size/length — is simply absent from this paper.

- **(c) RANKING error types by binary FFR≤0.80 decision-flip rate (per-case reclassification)**: **NOT COVERED.** This is the most decisive finding for T6. The paper does rank variables by IMPACT ON FFRCT UNCERTAINTY MAGNITUDE (standard deviation, pressure drop, flow rate) — MLD > boundary resistance > viscosity > lesion length — but this ranking is entirely in terms of continuous FFRCT variability/CI width compared against a measurement-reproducibility benchmark (±0.03), never in terms of a per-case/per-lesion binary reclassification count across the 0.80 threshold. No table or count of "N lesions/cases changed FFR category" appears anywhere; the closest approach (Fig. 7's illustrative LAD extrema spanning ~0.6 to >0.8) is explicitly caveated by the authors as an unrepresentative extremum of the stochastic space, not an actual uncertainty-driven reclassification rate, and is drawn from a single vessel in a single patient, not a cohort. T6's specific reclassification-rate metric, and the very idea of ranking distinct ERROR TYPES (not just variable categories) by that metric, is genuinely absent here.

- **(d) Testing whether RE-TUNING outlet BCs after a geometry change ABSORBS/MASKS the error**: **NOT COVERED.** Boundary resistance uncertainty is modeled as one independent, fixed-distribution stochastic axis (calibrated once from a separate 28-patient cohort) and is perturbed in parallel with, not in response to, geometry. There is no experiment in the paper where geometry is deliberately altered and BCs are subsequently re-solved/re-tuned to match a validation target; no tuned-vs-untuned comparison exists; and no statement anywhere claims or examines BC-tuning compensation for geometric error. The paper's only "compensation" mechanism discussed is a physical self-regulation effect internal to the flow physics (reduced flow through a narrowed segment lowering net pressure drop), which is conceptually and mechanistically distinct from a re-optimization/re-tuning of the resistance parameter itself.

**Bottom line — kill, wound, or leave intact?** This paper **wounds but does not kill** a headline built on ranking segmentation error types by FFR decision-flip rate — and it barely wounds it, because on the two dimensions that matter most (b) and (c) it is essentially silent, not competing. Sankaran et al. (2016) does establish, and would need to be cited as prior art for, the general finding that MLD/geometric uncertainty dominates over boundary-resistance and viscosity uncertainty in FFRCT variability, and it does use one real (inter-modality) measured-error dataset for MLD — so T6 cannot claim to be first to rank "geometry vs BC vs viscosity" or first to use any real measured geometric error data. But this paper (i) treats geometry as a single global MLD scalar plus a separate lesion-length scalar, with no eccentricity, side-branch, or taper/undersizing error types at all; (ii) reports only continuous FFRCT standard deviations/confidence intervals compared to a measurement-reproducibility benchmark, never a per-case binary reclassification count across the 0.80 cutoff; and (iii) never re-tunes boundary conditions after a geometry perturbation nor examines any masking/compensation mechanism via BC tuning. The independent reviewer's warning is only partially borne out: THIS specific 2016 JBiomech paper does not pre-empt T6's core (b)+(c)+(d) contributions, though its very similarly-titled and closely-related 2015 companion papers by the same authors (Sankaran, Grady, Taylor, 2015a "Fast computation of hemodynamic sensitivity to lumen segmentation uncertainty," IEEE TMI, and 2015b "Impact of geometric uncertainty on hemodynamic simulations using machine learning," CMAME) are explicitly flagged in this paper's own text as the closer prior geometric-uncertainty-only precedents and were NOT the paper assigned for this extraction — they remain the more urgent items to check before treating T6's novelty gate as cleared on contributions (b) and (c).
