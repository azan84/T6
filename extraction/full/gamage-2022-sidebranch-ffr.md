## Problem & claim
- Imaging modality and domain: Intravascular optical coherence tomography (IVOCT)-reconstructed coronary artery geometry plus idealized stenosed-artery geometry; CFD hemodynamic simulation for functional (FFR) assessment of coronary stenosis.
- Task: Other — mechanistic/parametric CFD + electrical-circuit-analogy investigation of how the presence, diameter, and location of a coronary side branch alters CFD-derived FFR, validated qualitatively against invasive (pressure-wire) FFR in 2 patient-specific OCT models.
- Stated gap the paper attacks: "As OCT pullback apparently does not include the side branches' geometry, the effect of the side branches is essentially ignored [12,13], or to a certain degree [5] in most of these studies... However, in these studies, the comprehensive analysis of the influence of the side branches on the FFR estimation is still lacking."
- Central claim in one sentence: "The side branches located at the downstream of the stenosis led to a lower FFR value, while the ones at the upstream had a minimal impact on the FFR estimation. Side branches with a diameter larger than one third of the main vessel diameter are suggested to be considered for a proper FFR estimation."

## Method
- Architecture / algorithm: (1) Three idealized stenosed-artery CFD models — model 1 (no side branch), model 2 (one side branch upstream of stenosis), model 3 (one side branch downstream of stenosis) — built in ANSYS SpaceClaim/ICEM CFD, meshed with tetrahedra (~500,000 elements idealized, ~2,000,000 for patient-specific; 4-layer prism boundary layer, initial thickness 0.02 mm, growth rate 1.3; grid-independence check to <0.01% pressure error). (2) Steady-state incompressible Navier–Stokes solved in ANSYS CFX (finite-volume, second-order upwind, co-located grid); non-Newtonian Carreau viscosity model (µ∞=0.0035 Pa·s, µ0=0.25 Pa·s, λ=25 s, n=0.25); blood density 1050 kg/m³; laminar flow assumed. (3) Boundary conditions: fixed static inlet pressure (90 mmHg for idealized models; guide-catheter-measured for patient models) + zero-gradient inlet velocity; no-slip wall; at every outlet a resistance boundary condition P=Q·R using Ohm's-law-analogous lumped resistances derived from Olufsen's structured-tree model (asymmetric fractal vascular tree, daughter-vessel diameter scale factors α=0.9, β=0.5, length/diameter ratio γ=25, minimum terminal diameter Dmin=50 µm), with a hyperemia-correction factor of 0.24 applied to the resting resistance. (4) A parallel electrical-circuit analogy (voltage↔pressure, resistance↔flow resistance) is derived algebraically to explain the CFD results: stenosis modeled as nonlinear resistance R(q)=C1+C2q (from dP=C1q+C2q²); FFR=R1/(R(q)+R1) for branch-upstream or no-branch cases (Eq. 13); FFR=R1/(R(q)·R1/R2+R1+Rt) with Rt=R1R2/(R1+R2) for branch-downstream case (Eq. 15).
- Key technical novelty vs. closest prior work: explicit, matched-boundary-condition (same distal resistance imposed with/without side branch) comparison of upstream-vs-downstream side-branch placement, paired with a closed-form circuit derivation showing *why* location — not just presence — of the branch determines the FFR effect; contrasted with two prior studies ([33], angiography-based, and [27], OCT+angiography fusion) that used non-matched boundary conditions (tuned distal resistance per model, or forced equal inlet mass flow) and reached different conclusions about branch significance.
- Loss functions, training regime, augmentation, pre-training: NOT APPLICABLE — this is a CFD/analytical study, no learned model.
- Inputs/outputs, dimensionality: Input — 3D STL vessel geometries (idealized parametric geometry; OCT-segmented lumen + side-branch-ostium contours lofted into 3D for 2 real patients) plus inlet pressure and outlet resistance boundary conditions. Output — steady 3D pressure/velocity fields; scalar FFR = distal outlet pressure / inlet (aortic) pressure; flowrate through defined vessel cross-sections.

## Data
- Datasets used: 3 idealized geometric models (parametric, not from imaging) + 2 real patient-specific vessel-tree models reconstructed from IVOCT pullback (frequency-domain ILUMIEN OCT system, St. Jude Medical; 2.7-Fr Dragonfly catheter; pullback speed 36 mm/s; axial resolution 20 µm) plus angiography-confirmed catheter position. Private clinical cases; no public dataset; no licence stated.
- Idealized geometry specifics (verbatim): "The length and diameter of the main vessel are 75 mm and 3 mm, respectively... Stenosis is spread over a length (L) of 10 mm, and the minimum lumen area of the stenosis is 20% of the healthy lumen area. The side branch has a diameter of 2.5 mm, connects to the main vessel at an angle of 75 degrees, located 25 mm away from the center of the stenosis, at the upstream considering the flow direction." (Model 3 uses the same side-branch geometry but placed downstream instead of upstream — the paper does not restate separate downstream-location/diameter numbers; only the categorical upstream↔downstream placement is varied for the single 2.5 mm branch.)
- Annotation protocol: Main lumen segmented with MATLAB image-processing toolbox (2020b); each side-branch lumen (near ostium) manually segmented separately, then lofted, combined in ANSYS SpaceClaim, and side branches extended for numerical convergence. Small branches (<0.5 mm) were excluded (stated in Limitations). No inter-annotator/agreement metric reported (single segmentation per patient, no repeat-reader study).
- Train/val/test split: NOT APPLICABLE (2-case validation study, no ML train/test split). External validation: the 2 OCT patient models are validated against each patient's own invasively measured (pressure-wire) FFR — not an independent/external cohort beyond these 2 cases.

## Evaluation
- Metrics used, with exact reported values (verbatim, Sections 3.1–3.2):
  - Idealized models — flow: "Models 1 and 2 had a similar outlet flowrate from the main vessel (outlet1), i.e., <0.01% difference." "...the outlet flowrate of the main vessel in model 3 was 15% lower than the other models." "...blood flow through the stenosis in model 3 (inlet) was 27% higher than the other models (outlet1)."
  - Idealized models — FFR: "The estimated FFR of model 2 was slightly less than model 1 (with a difference of 0.002)." "The estimated FFR in model 3 was significantly smaller, which was 15.5% lower compared to the other two models, suggesting that the side branch located at the downstream has a significant impact on FFR estimation."
  - Analytical diameter-sensitivity claim (not a CFD sweep, derived from Eq. 15/Murray's law): "the term R1/R2 decreases as the diameter of the side branch decreases and becomes negligible (<4%) if the side branch diameter is less than one third of the main vessel diameter."
  - Patient-specific models — FFR: "In patient 1, the side branches at the upstream of the stenosis did not contribute to a significant difference in FFR estimation." "For patient 2, the FFR value decreased by 13% when the side branch flow was considered. This difference was more significant than the one for patient 1, which was 2%."
  - Murray's-law cross-check: "The flow distribution derived using Murray's law showed good agreement with the simulated results, where the simulated flowrates were slightly lower (on average by ~7%)."
  - No absolute FFR values (e.g., "FFR = 0.7X") are given anywhere in the extracted text for any of the 5 models — all reported FFR results are relative differences/percentages between paired models.
- Baselines compared against: model-1 (no branch) as reference for idealized comparisons; each patient's own invasive/pressure-wire-measured FFR as reference for the 2 patient-specific models (qualitative visual agreement in Figure 7, no correlation coefficient, Bland–Altman, or numeric error vs. invasive FFR reported in the extracted text); Murray's-law-predicted flow distribution as a second analytical baseline.
- Statistical testing / CIs / ablations: NOT REPORTED — no confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, or formal statistical comparison; the idealized-model design is itself a 3-way structural ablation (no-branch / upstream / downstream), and the patient analysis is a with/without-side-branch ablation on the same base geometry.
- Compute reported: Mesh sizes given (~500,000 elements idealized; ~2,000,000 patient-specific); no GPU/CPU hardware, wall-clock runtime, or solver iteration counts reported.

## Reproducibility

## Limitations
- Limitations the authors admit (Section 4, verbatim): "The curvature of the arteries is not considered in the current geometry reconstruction process, which may deviate the FFR computations. However, previous studies reported minor effects on FFR caused by the curvature of the coronary arteries." "The side vessels are manually segmented and connected to the main vessel, where very small branches (<0.5 mm) were ignored. The errors in segmented side branch diameters, angles, and locations may deviate the flow distribution into side branches. However, these errors are likely to cause a minimum effect on FFR computation and have less impact on the FFR comparisons in the study." "The microvascular resistances of the side branch outlets were calculated by assuming a structured tree model connected to the outlets. While these resistance values are likely to vary from the realistic patient-specific microvascular resistances, they provide a reasonable approximation for the aim of the study." "The study only incorporated two patient-specific vessel models to evaluate the conclusions derived from the idealized models. However, the qualitative findings are expected to remain the same. Analysis of FFR in more patient-specific vessels will further strengthen the findings of the study."
- Limitations I can see that they do NOT admit: (1) No systematic parametric sweep of side-branch diameter or location was ever run in CFD — only one fixed diameter (2.5 mm, 83% of the 3 mm main-vessel diameter) at one fixed location (25 mm from stenosis center) was simulated in each of the two idealized branch models; the headline "one third diameter" rule is an algebraic asymptotic argument from Murray's-law scaling of R1, not an empirically fitted or CFD-validated threshold. (2) No side branch was ever fully removed from an otherwise-identical idealized geometry with the branch resistance redistributed — the idealized "no branch" model (model 1) is a wholly separate base geometry rather than model 2/3 with the branch topologically deleted, so the idealized arm never directly emulates a "was-there, now-missing" segmentation error. (3) The only true "branch present vs. branch absent on the same base geometry" comparison is in the 2 real patients, and only n=2 is used, both from the same cohort/institution, with no report of how many total side branches or what their diameter/location distribution was in either patient beyond qualitative description ("<1 mm" for patient 1's smallest branch). (4) No quantitative comparison (correlation, RMSE, Bland-Altman) of simulated FFR against the invasive FFR ground truth is given in the extracted text — only qualitative visual "good agreement"/"better agreement" language (Figure 7), so the magnitude of real-world disagreement this generates is not quantified. (5) No WSS, OSI, or any spatially resolved hemodynamic field is actually computed or reported by this study — WSS/atherosclerosis-risk relevance is cited only via other authors' studies ([26],[27]) as motivation, never computed here. (6) The outlet resistance re-tuning question is answered only for the *main-vessel* distal outlet, held deliberately fixed for the with/without-branch comparison ("The same distal resistance value was imposed when the model was considered without side branches") — this was a controlled-comparison choice for isolating branch effect, not a test of what happens under standard/realistic re-tuning workflow (e.g., re-deriving distal resistance from myocardial-mass allometry or from a fresh pressure-wire equalization after the branch is missed); the paper explicitly contrasts this fixed-resistance choice against a prior study [33] that instead retuned resistances separately per model and got a different (opposite) conclusion about branch significance — implying the answer is itself BC-protocol-dependent, a sensitivity the paper flags qualitatively but never quantifies.

## Openings
- Explicit "future work" statements, quoted: "More accurate segmentation of the side branches can be achieved by the fusion of OCT images and angiography views or through automated segmentation methods." "Analysis of FFR in more patient-specific vessels will further strengthen the findings of the study." "The use of morphometric flow rules to correct the flow in the main vessel can serve as a reasonable approximation, which can be combined with previously suggested equation-based FFR estimation methods to improve their performance."
- 3–6 unresolved questions this paper leaves open, each one sentence:
  1. Whether a systematic sweep of side-branch diameter (not just the single 2.5 mm/one-third-diameter analytical cutoff) and location distance from the stenosis produces a continuous, quantifiable dose-response curve for FFR error, rather than the three discrete categorical configurations tested here.
  2. Whether re-tuning (rather than holding fixed) the distal/main-vessel outlet resistance after a side branch is added or removed changes or reverses the direction/magnitude of the reported FFR effect, given the paper's own observation that a prior study using per-model-tuned resistances reached the opposite conclusion.
  3. Whether the FFR changes reported here (0.002 idealized-upstream, 15.5% idealized-downstream, 2% patient-1, 13% patient-2) are ever large enough, in a broader case series, to flip a stenosis across the clinical FFR ≤ 0.80 decision threshold — never tested with only 2 real cases and no absolute FFR values reported.
  4. Whether a fully missing (never-detected/never-segmented) side branch — as opposed to a branch whose flow is deliberately included vs. excluded in an already-correct segmentation — behaves identically to the with/without comparisons run here, since both patient models already had every branch properly identified from OCT before one was suppressed in silico.
  5. Whether WSS/OSI fields are materially altered by the same upstream/downstream branch configurations shown to alter FFR, since this paper cites the WSS relevance of side branches from other work but never computes it itself.

## Triage
- verdict: FULL
- reason: Closest prior work to T6's headline missed-side-branch error type; decisive for calibrating novelty claim.

## T6 decisive questions

**1. Exactly what side-branch variations were simulated.**
Only three idealized configurations, each a single fixed geometry, not a parameter sweep: "Three idealized artery models—without side branch, with one side branch at the upstream of the stenosis, and with one side branch at the downstream of the stenosis—were constructed." The one side branch used in both branch-bearing idealized models has a single fixed diameter and location: "The side branch has a diameter of 2.5 mm, connects to the main vessel at an angle of 75 degrees, located 25 mm away from the center of the stenosis." No idealized run varies diameter or distance continuously — diameter sensitivity is argued only analytically via the R1/R2 term in Eq. 15, concluding it "becomes negligible (<4%) if the side branch diameter is less than one third of the main vessel diameter." In the 2 real patients, the comparison is directly "with and without side branches" on the same reconstructed geometry (Figure 6 top vs. bottom) — this is the one case in the paper that is a true branch-present-vs-branch-absent (i.e., "missed branch") comparison, but it uses each patient's own real, heterogeneous set of branches (patient 1: three branches upstream of a diffuse stenosis plus one <1 mm branch after it; patient 2: branches downstream of a focal stenosis), not a systematically varied single branch. No idealized branch was ever "entirely removed" from a branch-bearing base geometry with resistance redistribution — model 1 (no branch) is a separately constructed geometry, not model 2/3 with the branch topologically deleted.

**2. Every reported numeric FFR value or delta, and the upstream/downstream asymmetry.**
Idealized: upstream branch (model 2) vs. no branch (model 1): FFR difference of 0.002, called "insignificant." Downstream branch (model 3) vs. the other two: "15.5% lower," called "significant." Flow evidence for the asymmetry: outlet1 flow model 1 vs. 2 differs by "<0.01%," while model 3's outlet1 flow is "15% lower than the other models" and flow through the stenosis in model 3 is "27% higher than the other models (outlet1)." Patient-specific: patient 1 (branches upstream) — "did not contribute to a significant difference in FFR estimation," quantified later as "2%." Patient 2 (branches downstream) — "FFR value decreased by 13% when the side branch flow was considered." So the asymmetry is roughly a 6.5x ratio in the two idealized-vs-patient comparable pairs (0.002 idealized-upstream ≈ negligible vs. 15.5% idealized-downstream; 2% patient-upstream vs. 13% patient-downstream) — consistently, downstream branches produce a materially larger FFR effect than upstream branches. No absolute FFR values (e.g., "FFR = 0.75") appear anywhere in the extracted text; every number is a relative percentage or absolute delta between paired models.

**3. Does the paper report any configuration crossing the 0.80 decision threshold?**
NOT REPORTED. The only appearance of "0.8" in the whole text is a citation of prior literature's diagnostic accuracy ("~85–87%) in classifying the vessels conferring to the diagnostic threshold (FFR ≤ 0.8)") describing other studies, not this paper's own results. This paper never states an absolute FFR value for any of its 5 models (3 idealized, 2 patient with-branch/without-branch pairs), so it cannot and does not say whether any configuration moved a case across 0.80. Threshold-crossing is never reported.

**4. Outlet boundary conditions — recomputed or held fixed when a branch changes?**
The electrical-analogy resistance network is the structured-tree model of Olufsen: "At each outlet, a resistance-based boundary condition was imposed... P = Pout = Qout·Rout" with Ri = 128µγ/(πdi³) per segment (Eq. 9) and parallel-combination rule 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 (Eq. 10), parameters α=0.9, β=0.5, γ=25, Dmin=50 µm, hyperemic scaling factor 0.24. Decisive procedure for the patient-specific with/without-branch comparison, quoted verbatim: "For the patient-specific models, the resistances at each side branch were determined using the structured tree approach, while the resistance at the distal end of the main vessel was determined to match the pressure wire measurement. **The same distal resistance value was imposed when the model was considered without side branches.**" So the main-vessel distal outlet resistance is deliberately HELD FIXED (not re-tuned/re-derived) when the side branch is removed — this isolates the branch's hemodynamic effect for the comparison, but is explicitly not a re-calibration-to-clinical-target workflow. The paper contrasts this against ref [33] (Gosling et al.), which "tuned separately to match the measured FFR values" for models with and without flow leakage and reported the opposite conclusion (branch effect insignificant) — the paper attributes the discrepancy directly to this boundary-condition-tuning choice: "This is different from our findings; this is mainly due to the different generic distal resistance values used for the models with and without flow leakage, tuned separately to match the measured FFR values. In contrast, the current study imposed the same distal resistance at the main vessel exit in models with and without side branches for comparison purposes."

**5. Idealised, OCT-reconstructed, or both? Real cases and invasive FFR ground truth?**
Both. Three idealized parametric models plus two OCT-reconstructed patient-specific models ("two representative patient-specific models"). Invasive/pressure-wire FFR ground truth exists for both real patients (Section 2.3, "Clinical FFR Measurement": PressureWire X Guidewire, adenosine-induced hyperemia, FFR = ratio of distal to aortic pressure) and is used as the comparison target in Figure 7 ("The simulated FFR variation was compared against measurements derived from pressure wire pullbacks"), but only qualitative agreement language is given in the extracted text ("showed good agreement," "showed a better agreement"), with no numeric correlation/error metric against the invasive values reported.

**6. WSS/OSI or spatially resolved fields, and branch sensitivity?**
NOT REPORTED as a computed result of this study. WSS is mentioned only in the Discussion as a cited motivation from other authors' work: "The alterations in the velocity distributions caused by side branches can lead to changes in local hemodynamics parameters, such as wall shear stress (WSS), and further induce remodeling of the vessel wall. Previous studies [26,27] have revealed the importance of the inclusion of side branches when assessing the risk of atherosclerosis development in relation to WSS distribution." OSI is never mentioned anywhere in the text. This study itself reports only pressure and velocity distribution figures and the scalar FFR/flowrate outputs — no WSS or OSI field is computed or quantified here.

**7. Stated limitations and future work, verbatim.**
See "Limitations" section above for the full Section 4 quotes (curvature not modeled; manual side-branch segmentation with branches <0.5 mm ignored, errors "likely to cause a minimum effect on FFR computation"; structured-tree microvascular resistances "likely to vary from the realistic patient-specific microvascular resistances" but "a reasonable approximation"; only two patient-specific models, "qualitative findings are expected to remain the same," more cases would "further strengthen the findings"). Future work (Discussion, quoted): "More accurate segmentation of the side branches can be achieved by the fusion of OCT images and angiography views [27] or through automated segmentation methods [34]." "The use of morphometric flow rules to correct the flow in the main vessel can serve as a reasonable approximation, which can be combined with previously suggested equation-based FFR estimation methods [5,13] to improve their performance."

**8. DECISIVE JUDGEMENT — how much of T6's missed-side-branch arm is already in print here?**
A meaningful but narrow slice is already established, and it should not be overstated. What Gamage et al. (2022) already establishes: (a) the qualitative direction and rough order of magnitude of the missed-side-branch effect — a branch downstream of a stenosis lowers FFR materially (15.5% in idealized geometry, 13% in one real patient) while an upstream branch has a much smaller effect (0.002 absolute / 2% in the matched idealized/real pairs) — giving T6 a directional prior and an existing analytical (circuit-equation) mechanism (Eqs. 13–15) for *why* location matters; (b) a stated, citable rule of thumb that branch diameter below ~1/3 of the main-vessel diameter is analytically negligible (<4% resistance-ratio contribution), useful as a plausibility check on any T6-injected branch-size thresholds; (c) explicit prior evidence that the boundary-condition-tuning protocol changes the conclusion: holding the main-vessel distal resistance fixed across the with/without-branch comparison (as Gamage did) versus re-tuning it separately per model (as Gosling et al. [33] did) flips the qualitative significance verdict — this is direct textual support for T6's planned BC re-tuning "absorption test," though Gamage never runs that re-tuning experiment itself, only cites the discrepancy with another paper's differently-tuned study.

What remains genuinely new for T6, and should not be claimed as already covered: (1) **Real disagreement-derived magnitudes** — Gamage's "missed branch" evidence is only 2 real patients, both already correctly segmented from OCT with all branches identified, then one branch selectively suppressed in silico; there is no distribution of missed-branch magnitudes from real reader/algorithm segmentation disagreement (e.g., how often and how severely automated or human segmentation actually misses a branch, and what geometric/flow error that produces) — T6's disagreement-derived magnitudes are entirely new. (2) **Decision-flip ranking against other error types** — Gamage never reports a single absolute FFR value, never mentions the 0.80 threshold in its own results, and therefore never determines whether any configuration flips a clinical decision, let alone ranks this error type's flip-rate against other segmentation-error types (e.g., stenosis over/under-sizing, lumen smoothing); this entire comparative/decision-flip axis is untouched and is squarely T6's contribution. (3) **BC re-tuning as an absorption/masking test** — Gamage holds resistance fixed by design for a clean mechanistic comparison and only *observes* (via a literature contrast, not its own experiment) that a different tuning choice elsewhere reversed the conclusion; it never runs a controlled ablation on the *same* geometry/data comparing fixed-vs-re-tuned outlet resistance to see whether re-tuning masks or absorbs the missed-branch-induced FFR error — this is a genuine gap T6 would fill. (4) The idealized side-branch geometry itself is a single point in diameter/location space (2.5 mm, 25 mm, 75°), not a systematic sweep, and there is no idealized model where a branch is topologically deleted from an otherwise-identical branch-bearing geometry with resistance redistribution — so even the "clean" mechanistic half of Gamage's evidence is coarser than what a dedicated missed-branch injection study (T6) would need. In short: Gamage is the closest and most citable prior mechanistic/directional study, and materially de-risks T6's expected qualitative finding (downstream >> upstream sensitivity) and motivates the BC-retuning arm, but it supplies no decision-threshold analysis, no cross-error-type ranking, no controlled BC-retuning ablation, and no distribution of real-world missed-branch magnitudes — all of which are left entirely open for T6.

**5-line summary:** Gamage et al. (2022, Appl. Sci.) is the closest prior work on missed side branches and FFR, running 3 idealized CFD models (no/upstream/downstream branch, fixed 2.5 mm diameter, 25 mm from stenosis) plus 2 real OCT patient models compared with/without branches against invasive pressure-wire FFR. It reports FFR changes of 0.002 (idealized upstream, negligible) vs. 15.5% (idealized downstream) and 2% (patient 1, upstream) vs. 13% (patient 2, downstream) — establishing a clear upstream/downstream asymmetry and an analytical "diameter < 1/3 of main vessel = negligible" rule, plus a decisive note that main-vessel outlet resistance was held fixed (not re-tuned) across the with/without comparison, with a literature contrast showing that per-model resistance re-tuning elsewhere reversed the conclusion. It never reports an absolute FFR value, never discusses the 0.80 threshold, never computes WSS/OSI, and never runs a systematic diameter/location sweep or a controlled fixed-vs-retuned BC ablation. T6's real-disagreement-derived magnitudes, cross-error-type decision-flip ranking, and BC-retuning absorption test are therefore all still open.
